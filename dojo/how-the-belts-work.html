    The Dojo

    How the belts work

    Two ditches and a road — and five belts ordered not by how badly somebody behaved, but by how much benefit of the doubt is honestly left.

    Two things to have before you start. Neither takes long and the second one is the reason this place is safe to use.

    Two ditches and a road

    Left is caving. You agree, you smooth it over, you say it is fine. It buys you a quiet evening.

    Right is lashing. You say the true thing with the edge left on it. It buys you the relief of not being a doormat.

    They look like opposites and they are not. Both are ways of protecting yourself — one from the awkwardness, one from feeling small. The road is neither, and it is not the average of them either. It is the response that costs you something for the other person's sake: true, said so that they can actually hear it, and leaving them a way back.

    Which is why it is usually the hardest of the three and never the most moderate. If a response gets easier as it gets better, check it again. Something has gone wrong.

    If the right ditch is the one you live in — if the edge is on the sentence before you have chosen to put it there, or if it never arrives at all until it arrives all at once — the chapter for it is Anger, in Keep Your Heart. Anger too large and anger that never comes are not opposite problems; they are the same signal failing the same way.

    If the left ditch is the one you live in — if agreeing is not a choice you make but something that happens before you have decided — it has a clinical name and a page of its own: Fawning, on My Toolbox. Fight, flight, freeze, and the fourth one, which gets mistaken for a personality. Worth knowing before you try to drill your way out of it.

    The belts

    The belts are not ranked by how badly the other person is behaving. They are ranked by how much benefit of the doubt the evidence will still bear — how much room is left to explain what happened kindly and still be honest.

    White — Nothing was aimed

    They were tired, clumsy, frightened, or in a hurry. The sting was real and the intent was simply not there. Most of what stings in an ordinary week lives here.
    Yellow — Aimed at the problem, not at you

    Ordinary pressure. A sharp ask, an argument pressed hard, somebody who wants a thing and is saying so. Uncomfortable, and entirely above board.
    Green — They are defending themselves badly

    Their own shame is driving it. Good faith underneath, real harm on top, and they cannot see the second because of the first.
    Brown — A pattern you can no longer explain charitably

    Repeated, shaped, and it lands in their favour every time. You still do not know their heart, and you are not required to guess at it in order to act.
    Black — The mask has fused

    They are no longer telling you a story. They are telling themselves one, and they believe it, which is why nothing you say reaches. Technique runs out here.

    In the actual art a black belt means a beginner — somebody who has finished the basics and can now start. That is about right.

    What moves a mask up a belt

    The same mask turns up at more than one belt. What moves it up is never the mask. It is what else is in the room, and there are two things that do it.

    A track record. The same move, again, landing the same way, and always in their favour. One of anything proves nothing. Six of a thing is a shape.

    An act that is out of bounds on its own. Something wrong in itself whatever was meant by it — a confidence repeated to other people, a checkable fact denied, somebody's standing moved as a punishment. These can raise a belt inside a single conversation, because you do not need a pattern to establish that a thing happened.

    Notice that both of those are acts. That is the whole safeguard. “You told three people what I said in confidence” is checkable, and it can be answered. “You are manipulative” is a verdict on an inside you have never seen and cannot see.

    So a higher belt changes how carefully you answer and what happens to somebody's keys. It never tells you what they are. Their heart stays shut to you at every belt on this page, and so does yours to them — which is why every case here ends with the same mask on your own face.

    Access moves on evidence. Standing does not move at all.

    That is the rule from the Map of the Keep, and everything in this yard is downstream of it. You don't lose your seat here. You might lose the spoon for a week.
