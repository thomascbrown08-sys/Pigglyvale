    The Mind-Reader

    Somebody tells you what is inside you, and then answers the thing they invented. You cannot win the argument, because it was never about a fact.

    Bruno has volunteered for this one and is enjoying it far too much. He has been asked to run it again twice, once for being too obviously villainous and once for laughing. Old Ambrose is on the wall with the honey. Nobody in Pigglyvale actually speaks like this, which is rather the point of a yard.

    Green belt. Somebody is defending themselves badly and their own shame is driving it. There is good faith underneath and there is real harm on top, and they cannot see the second because of the first.

    What makes this mask hard is not that it is cruel. It is that it puts the argument somewhere no argument can be won. Denial looks like proof. Agreement means you have accepted a version of yourself you do not hold. And forty minutes later you are still producing evidence about your own heart, which is the one subject on which evidence does not exist.

    Drill one — it is being done to you

    You have had a bad fortnight. Two deliveries went wrong, you have been at the bakery since four most mornings, and you have not been to the Long Table in eleven days.

    A friend catches you on the row. Not angry — that would be easier. Sad, and gentle about it, and already forgiving you.

    “It's alright. I know you've decided we're not worth the trouble any more.”

    What do you say?

    What happened next

    The relief on their face is immediate and it is real, and you feel the whole thing go soft, and the walk home is a great deal easier than it would otherwise have been.

    You go on the Thursday. It is a good evening.

    Three weeks later it happens again over something smaller, and the charge is larger, because the first one was granted and nobody disputed it. By harvest you have started arranging your week around whether they will be in a position to say it — not around whether you have wronged them, but around whether they will be able to feel that you have.

    Ambrose

    Now, hold on to what was good in that, because it was not nothing. You wanted them not to be in pain and you were prepared to be inconvenient to yourself about it. Keep that. It is the best thing you brought to the row today.

    But look at what you actually said. You apologised for something that had not happened. That is not humility and it is not peacemaking — it is a false statement made to buy a quiet evening, and the price of it was a fact. The record now shows that you agreed you had decided they were not worth the trouble. You will find that sentence waiting for you later, in their mouth, and you will not be able to take it back, because you put it there.

    Left ditch. Caving.

    What happened next

    There is a silence you have to sit inside, and it is longer than is comfortable, and you do not fill it.

    “You weren't at the Long Table,” they say.

    “No.”

    And then, smaller and quite differently: “I sat with the Bramblewicks. All four of them. For two hours.” A pause. “I don't like walking in on my own.”

    Which is the actual thing, and it was never once about what you had decided about them.

    Ambrose

    You refused the ground. That is the whole move and it is smaller than people expect — six words of denial, flat and unelaborated, and then a question about something that happened.

    Interiors are unarguable. Events are checkable. So when someone hands you a claim about your own heart, do not go looking for evidence, because there is none and you will be there all night. Ask what occurred. It nearly always turns out to be something small, factual and entirely reasonable, sitting underneath a sentence that was none of those things.

    Notice the cost as well, because it is real. You had to let the accusation stand unanswered for about four seconds while you asked about something else. Everything in you wanted to spend those seconds on your own defence. Not spending them is the price of this one.

    The road.

    What happened next

    They go very still. “Right,” they say. “Right. Fair enough.”

    And they go, and they are perfectly civil about it, and it is the last thing they bring you.

    In the autumn Marisol mentions, without meaning anything by it, that they had a hard month back in the summer.

    Ambrose

    Every word of that was defensible. It is true that another person's conclusions about you are not yours to carry, and there are rooms in which that sentence is exactly the right one.

    This was not one of them, and here is the trouble with it: you have proved their case. They arrived saying you have decided we are not worth the trouble, and you replied with a sentence that closed the door. They will not remember that they invented the charge. They will remember that you agreed with it. And you have lost the thing that was actually underneath, which you never found out.

    Right ditch. Lashing.

    What happened next

    “I know,” they say. “I know you've been busy.”

    Two beats. Then: “It's just that you'd have made time before.”

    So you explain about the second delivery. They say they are not blaming you. You explain about the four o'clock starts. They say of course, they understand, it's fine, honestly. You find yourself listing the eleven days one at a time, out loud, on Marketrow, to a friend, like a ledger.

    Forty minutes later you are both exhausted, everything you said was true, and nobody has mentioned the Bramblewicks or the walking in alone, because it never came up.

    Ambrose

    This is the one nearly everybody picks, and I want to be careful about it, because it is the honest answer. You did not cave and you did not lash. You told the truth and you had the facts on your side.

    And you lost anyway, because you agreed to argue on their ground. The moment you begin producing evidence about your own heart, you have conceded that your heart is the thing in question — and innocence has no proof. There is no quantity of four o'clock starts that demonstrates what you have or have not decided about somebody. You could have gone on until dark.

    It is the right answer to the wrong question. Do not answer the question. Move it.

    Not a ditch. The right answer to the wrong question.

    Run it again — the fourth one especially, if you have not.

    All four. Three of those were true. Only one of them found out what had actually happened, and it was the one that spent the least time on you.

    Now the other side of it

    Nobody knows another person's inside — which disqualifies you as well.

    Drill two — you are wearing it

    Thomas has been odd for three days. Short in the mornings, elsewhere in the afternoons, and he has not made a single joke about his own vocabulary, which for him is practically a fever.

    You have worked out why. On Tuesday you corrected him in front of Auntie Yolanda — only mildly, and you were right, but you did it in front of her. He is sulking about it.

    You are fairly sure. You are, in fact, quite sure.

    What do you do?

    What happened next

    He blinks at you from a considerable height.

    “Tuesday?”

    It comes out slowly and in the wrong order, the way these things do. His sister in Snouton has been ill since Saturday. He has not said anything because there is nothing anybody can do and he did not want to be a weight in the kitchen during a bad week.

    And now he has your apology to deal with as well — and, worse, he has just been told that something happened on Tuesday that he ought perhaps to have minded. You have handed him a grievance he did not have. He is far too kind to pick it up in front of you, and he will think about it later.

    Ambrose

    You apologised to your own theory. And your theory was not idle — you built it out of three days of real evidence, correctly observed. He was short. He was elsewhere. Every fact you had was true.

    The invention was the why, and it was invisible to you as an invention, because it arrived feeling exactly like noticing. That is the whole of this mask and it is why it fits so well on people who are paying attention. It does not feel like guessing. It feels like knowing somebody well.

    An apology for a thing that did not happen is not a small kindness with a wrong address on it. It plants something. He will be turning Tuesday over tonight, and Tuesday was fine until you went and made it a subject.

    This is the mask. On you. Wearing a very kind face.

    What happened next

    “My sister is ill,” he says.

    That is the whole of it. It takes four seconds. You sit down and he tells you about Snouton and the physician and the letter that has not come, and at no point does anybody mention Tuesday, because Tuesday was not a thing.

    Ambrose

    Notice how little you had to be right about. You reported the behaviour — quiet, since Tuesday, both of which you had actually witnessed — and then you stopped, and let him supply the part that was his to supply.

    That is the whole discipline: say what you saw, ask what it means, and do not fill the gap. The gap is unbearable and it is about a second and a half long. The proverb is blunt about it — answering before you have heard is folly and shame (Proverbs 18:13) — and it is not talking about interrupting. It is talking about arriving with the answer already made.

    The road. Two clauses long.

    What happened next

    He brings it on the Friday, unprompted, and it is his sister, and he apologises for having been poor company.

    So it came right. But you spent four days with a story running quietly underneath everything — and you had been slightly cool with him on the Wednesday, because you were managing a sulk that did not exist. He noticed that. He put it down to the bad week, and he was wrong about it in exactly the way you were wrong about him.

    Ambrose

    Patience is a real virtue and I am not going to talk you out of it. Waiting for somebody to bring a thing themselves is often right, and it is nearly always right when the thing is theirs.

    But you were not waiting emptily. You were waiting with a theory, and a theory does not sit still — it gets into your Wednesday. Silence with a story running underneath is not patience. It is a verdict that has not been read out yet. If you are going to wait, wait without the answer, which is much harder than it sounds and is the actual skill.

    Sometimes the road. Not this time.

    What happened next

    The feathers on the back of his head go up.

    “I am not sulking,” he says, with enormous dignity, and then does not tell you about his sister, because you have just made the conversation about whether he is a person who sulks.

    He tells you on the Sunday. It is stiffer than it should have been, and he says I did not wish to burden the kitchen, which is a sentence with a bruise in it.

    Ambrose

    You did the same thing they did to you on the row this morning, and I do not say that to be clever about it. You named his interior — sulking — and then asked him to respond to your name for it. There is no good answer to that. Denying it looks like sulking. Admitting it means accepting a word he would not have chosen.

    And you may well have been right. That is not the point and it never is. Being right about somebody's inside does not give you the standing to say it for them, because they cannot check your work and neither can you.

    Right ditch, and the mask, at the same time.

    Run it again. The first one is the one worth sitting with.

    All four. The kind option and the sharp option failed for the same reason, which is the useful thing to take away from this belt. Both of them arrived with the answer already made.

    Taking it off

    This mask does not feel like guessing. It feels like knowing somebody well — and often you do know them well, and your reading is right four times in five. That is exactly what makes it hard to catch. It is built out of real attention, and the attention is a virtue.

    So the question is not do I assume too much about people. Everyone answers that generously. The question is narrower:

    The last time you knew why somebody was being off with you — did you say the why out loud as though it were a fact? And did you ever find out?

    Most of the time we never find out, which is why the habit survives. The theory is never tested, so it is never disproved, so it hardens into a thing you know about that person.

    The repair is not thinking less about people. It is one small surgery on the sentence: keep what you saw, cut what you concluded, and leave the gap for them.

    “You've been quiet since Tuesday. Is something wrong?”
    — not —
    “You've been sulking since Tuesday.”

    And when you have already said the second one, which you will, the way back is short and it does not require much:

    “I decided I knew why you were quiet, and I said it as though it were a fact. It wasn't — it was a guess, and I hadn't asked. What is actually going on?”

    The whole of the discipline is in one line and it is worth learning by heart, because it works on both sides of the row: report what you saw, ask what it means, and do not fill the gap.

    Practise it

    1. Take the last thing you were sure somebody was thinking about you. Write the sentence you would have said. Now cross out every word that was not something you watched happen. What is left is usually shorter and always safer.
    2. Say drill one's second answer out loud — “I haven't, and something's clearly wrong. Tell me what happened.” — and notice how badly you want to add a sentence of defence after it. Do not add it. That is the drill.
    3. The harder one. Somebody has read your interior wrongly and said so, and you never corrected it, because correcting it seemed like a fight. Do they still believe it?
