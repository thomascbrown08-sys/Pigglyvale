    A Chart from the Kingdom

    The Map of the Keep

    Six rooms, working inward, and one mark that can be set anywhere. Carolyn drew it on the back of a bread list, the Friday after Fair Week.

    Every kingdom is a set of rooms, and every room has a door, and the doors are not all the same door. This is obvious the moment somebody says it out loud and almost nobody says it out loud, which is how people end up handing a stranger the key to the innermost room on a Tuesday and then wondering, later, what happened.

    So: the Keep. Six rooms, working inward.

    The six rooms

    6 · The Road. Where everyone is. You are civil to people on the Road. You do not owe them anything else. Most of the world lives here and that is not an insult — it is the correct place for most of the world.

    5 · Marketrow. People you deal with. There is an exchange going on: goods, work, a service, a favour that gets returned. Marketrow is warm and busy and it is not intimate. You can like somebody enormously and keep them here for years.

    4 · The Great Hall. People who are part of the life of the place. They come to things. They know your business roughly. You would notice if they stopped coming — and, importantly, they would notice if you did.

    3 · The Inner Court. People who are asked. Their judgement is wanted, their opinion changes decisions, and they hear a thing before it is announced. This is the ring most people never think to draw, and it is the one that does the most work.

    2 · The Long Table. The few. They get the unfinished version of you: the tired sentence, the thing you have not worked out yet, the part that is still being built. This is a very small number of people and it is meant to be.

    1 · The Kitchen. One or two, if that. Some people have nobody here and are not thereby broken.

    The rule the whole chart hangs on

    Blood gets you the seat. Conduct keeps the keys.

    Standing and access are two different things, and confusing them is where nearly all the damage gets done. Standing is whether you belong here at all — whether there is a place at the table with your name on it. That is given, and it is not withdrawn as a punishment, and if it can be taken away from you when somebody is cross then it was never standing in the first place. Access is which rooms you are in. That moves. It moves on evidence, and it moves both ways, and it is supposed to.

    So the sentence to hold onto is: you don’t lose your seat here. You might lose the spoon for a week.

    How anybody moves

    Inward, one room at a time, on evidence. That is the whole method and it is very boring, which is why people skip it.

    Every step inward is a small thing done and seen: a promise kept when it was inconvenient, a confidence held, a hard sentence said kindly to your face instead of pleasantly behind your back. Small things arrive one at a time. There is no way to have a great many of them all at once. Which means a person’s standing with you cannot honestly be built faster than the small things arrive, however much you like them.

    And a step outward is the same evidence running the other way. Not a punishment. Not a verdict on their soul. Just: this is what has actually been shown, and here is where that puts us for now.

    The skip

    Here is what actually goes wrong, and it is almost never a villain.

    Somebody arrives who is delightful. They are funny and capable and easy to be around and they turn up in a week you badly needed them. And because the pull of that is real, they get moved four rings inward in a fortnight — not by a decision anybody would defend out loud, but by a series of small warm moments that never got counted.

    Nothing is underneath the move. No small things were done and seen, because there was no time for any to happen. So the first real weight that comes on — the first disagreement, the first awkward Tuesday, the first thing that needs somebody to be careful with what they were told — goes straight through the floor, and they end up back at the outer edge, usually with the whole thing looking like their fault.

    It was not their fault. Nobody built the stairs.

    The other half of the same arithmetic is quieter and worse. While the four-ring leap is happening, somebody who has been doing the small things reliably for twenty years is still exactly where they were — because reliability makes no noise, and nothing that makes no noise ever gets added up.

    The Watch

    One mark, and it can be set on any room.

    It means: this person is welcome here, and I am not lending them anything. It is what you do when somebody has shown you something real and you have forgiven them completely and you are still not going to hand them the cart. Forgiveness is immediate and it is yours alone to give. Trust is evidence and it takes two. Those are not in a fight with each other, whatever it feels like at three in the morning.

    The Watch is not a sentence with an end date. It comes off the same way anything moves: on small things, done and seen.

    That is the whole chart. It is not a system for keeping people out. It is a way of being able to say yes properly, because a yes only means something if a no was available.
