# Arc D — the Ward. Planning brief.

**Status: not to be written yet.** This document exists so that when it is written, it is written on purpose.
It is a brief for a *dedicated planning session*, not a spine. Nothing here is canon.

Related: `the-arc-room_v2.md` §6 already logs **Arc D, the ward** as *later, told in advance*. Whatever is
sketched there is the starting point and this brief sits on top of it.

---

## 1. What the arc is

Carolyn takes in an orphan. The arc runs long — the raising, the ordinary difficulty of it, the love that
develops, and the particular satisfaction of turning out to be a good mother to somebody who was not
expected.

## 2. What it is quietly for, stated plainly so nobody stumbles into it

The author's hope is that watching Carolyn give a child a secure place may do something, obliquely, for a
reader whose own experience was of being *in* a household without being securely *of* it. The device is
borrowed from narrative work — seeing your own story from another angle, wearing fiction so it can be
approached at all.

**This is written down so a future session does not blunder into it, not so it can be aimed at.** The arc is
never to be steered by it. See the guards below.

## 3. The four guards. None of these are optional.

**1. Point of view stays locked to Carolyn.** Never once inside the child's head. The intent is that the
reader sits with the one *giving* the secure place, not the one receiving it — and if she ends up sitting
with the child, the arc has put her back in the chair of the person waiting to be chosen, which is the
opposite of what it is for. Authorial intent does not control identification. Point of view mostly does.

**2. It is a good story that happens to carry this, never a therapy wearing a story.** If the reader can
feel that an arc was built to treat her, she becomes a patient, and that reaches backwards and changes what
the whole site has been. The existing rule already covers it and must be enforced without exception here:
**the site never narrates her progress.** The arc offers an experience. It never comments on it.

**3. The child is a character, not a function.** They need wants that have nothing to do with the theme — a
grudge, a favourite tool, an opinion about a vegetable, a friendship the adults find inconvenient. If the
child exists to be a mirror, readers feel it within two episodes.

**4. Write it so it is worth having even if it changes nothing.** The therapeutic hope is a hope. The story
must be justified by being a good story. Building it to produce a specific interior result loads a
disappointment onto a gift.

## 4. Why it is a genuinely good story anyway

Worth stating, because if the only argument for the arc is the hidden one, the arc will be sentimental.

**Carolyn's flaw is the engine.** She says yes too fast, absorbs costs silently, and cannot bear to
disappoint. A child is the one relationship where that does maximum damage and cannot be hidden — you cannot
manage a child by absorbing, because they are there the next morning. Every failure mode the series has been
building for two seasons arrives at once and in a form she cannot outrun.

**And Thomas's flaw doubles it.** He fixes. A child in the kitchen is an unlimited supply of things to fix,
and every one he fixes is one the child does not learn to do.

## 5. The beat to build toward, which is not the one you would expect

Not the taking-in. Not a rescue, not a decision, not a courtroom.

**An ordinary Tuesday, eight or nine months in, where nothing happens.** The child is simply there,
unremarkable, arguing about vegetables, bored, secure enough to be dull. Nobody remarks on it. Carolyn
notices it for about four seconds and then gets on with the beans.

*Security is boring.* That is the whole payload, and it is the opposite of a climax, which is why it needs
planning rather than instinct. Everything else in the arc exists to make that Tuesday land.

## 6. Canon conflict that must be resolved first

**The Abandonment Protocol.** The bible currently requires abandonment to be approached *obliquely* and
*resolved within the same episode*. A ward arc is a sustained, multi-episode engagement with exactly that.

The rule was written for a good reason and this arc breaks it. It must be **amended deliberately**, with new
limits written down — not quietly overrun. The planning session should produce the amendment before it
produces a spine. Candidate shape: oblique remains the default; the ward arc is a named, bounded exception
with its own rule about how long a fear may stand unresolved on the page.

## 7. What has to be earned first — criteria, not a season number

The arc cannot run until all of these are true. Check them honestly.

- [ ] **The reader has watched Carolyn fail and be forgiven repeatedly.** Otherwise the mothering reads as
      idealised and the arc becomes a fantasy rather than a possibility.
- [ ] **The long clock is established.** Growth has visibly taken latency, cost, and failure-that-does-not-reset
      elsewhere in the series. Without that, a child arriving and things going well reads as a fix.
- [ ] **Carolyn can say no.** If she still cannot, the child arrives and the arc becomes about her flaw
      rather than about the child, and the flaw swallows it.
- [ ] **The repair rhythm is trusted.** The reader must already believe that when Carolyn fails somebody, it
      gets named and mended — because she *will* fail the child, and that has to be survivable on the page.
- [ ] **At least one long-arc payoff has already landed**, so the series has demonstrated it can carry
      something across a season and pay it properly.

Season One is too early. Season Two probably is as well. That is a feature.

## 8. Open questions for the planning session

- **Is the ward Donna?** A young deer taken into a kingdom of pigs is visibly not-of-this-family, which puts
  belonging-without-matching directly on the page. It also uses a character already asked for. Risk: too
  on-the-nose, and it overloads a character who may be wanted for something else.
- **Where do they come from, and is anyone still alive?** This is the single largest decision. A dead parent
  and a living-but-absent one are entirely different arcs and only one of them is writable here.
- **Does the kingdom decide, or does Carolyn?** The Long Table has a claim. This is also the cleanest way to
  make it a *story* rather than a private emotional event.
- **How does it end, and does it end?** The arc room's habit is to know the last beat first.
- **Butterflies.** This arc will generate many candidate moments. They must stay rare or they stop meaning
  anything. Decide the budget in advance — probably two or three across the whole arc.
- **What does Thomas do?** He has no hands and cannot pick a child up. That is either a running joke or
  something quietly devastating, and it should be chosen rather than discovered.

## 10. A note for whoever picks this up

The reader this is for is a real person and the author is not her therapist, and knows it. The site's whole
posture elsewhere is that it sits *alongside* support rather than replacing it. This arc does not change
that and must not be written as though it does.

The most useful thing a story can do here is not to fix anything. It is to put a true picture where she can
see it, and then be quiet about it.
