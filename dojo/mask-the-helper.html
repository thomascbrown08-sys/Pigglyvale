    The Dojo · A Mask

    The Helper

    “I was protecting you.” — love with a fault in it, and the one most people are wearing without knowing.

    Thomas has agreed to wear this one on the grounds that it is his and he ought to know it best. Bruno has been told he may not laugh.

    Somebody does a hard thing for you. Kindly, well, and without asking. They are pleased about it, and they are pleased for you, and every part of that is sincere.

    And something has been taken, and you cannot immediately say what, and the not being able to say what is the whole difficulty of it.

    What is under it

    “I was protecting you.”

    What it protects underneath
    Not you. Them. Watching somebody you love struggle is genuinely painful, and standing there useless while it happens is worse. The mask lifts both at once.
    What it buys
    Real goods, and they should be said plainly. The hard thing is done. You are spared an afternoon of dread. And they get to be useful to somebody they love, which is one of the better feelings available to a person.
    Why it fails
    It answers a need they have and charges it to you. And what it takes is not the errand — it is the evidence. You were about to find out you could do it. Now you will not, and next time the dread will be the same size or bigger, because nothing has happened to shrink it.
    What Scripture puts in its place
    Paul says bear one another's burdens, and then four verses later that each will have to bear his own load — which looks like a contradiction until you notice he has used two different words. The first is a crushing weight, more than one person can carry. The second is a man's own pack, the ordinary size of thing he is meant to walk under. The command is to get under what would crush somebody. It is not to carry their pack, and doing it anyway is not obedience to the first verse but disobedience to the second. Lifting a burden is love. Lifting a load says you could not have. — Galatians 6:2, 6:5

    Taking it off yourself

    Almost nobody wearing this one thinks they are. It does not feel like taking. It feels like love, and the reason it feels like love is that most of it is love. That is what makes it a mask rather than a lie — the wearer is the first one fooled.

    The useful question is narrow. Not do I help too much, which nobody answers honestly, but: when you stepped in, did you ask her first — and if you didn't, what were you afraid would happen if you had? The answer is usually she would have said no, and then I would have had to stand there and watch.

    Which means the repair is not helping less. It is one sentence, before: “Would you like me to, or would you rather do it yourself?”

    Where it turns up

    This one sits low and stays low. It is worn by people acting in good faith, and it does not climb the belts easily — a helper who will not stop when asked has stopped being a helper and is wearing something else.

    White — nothing was aimed

    They could see you were dreading it, and they were right, and they did not ask.
    Yellow

    Not built. This mask rarely arrives under pressure.
    Green — when they cannot stop

    In preparation. The helper who has been asked to ask, and keeps forgetting, because stopping costs them the only role they are sure of.
    Brown

    Not offered. At this belt the helping is no longer the point of it, and what you are looking at is the Ledger or the Turn.
    Black

    Not offered.
