    Under the Mind-Reader

    What it protects, what it buys, why it fails — and what it looks like on your own face.

    What is under it

    “I know how you really feel about me.”

    What it protects underneath
    Nearly always a fear that has already been half-confirmed by something small and real. They were alone at the table for two hours. That part happened. The mask goes over the top of it so the fear can be spoken without being risked — because a fear stated as a question might be answered, and an answer might be no.
    What it buys
    It gets the frightening thing said out loud with the frightening part removed. It arrives pre-forgiven, so it cannot be called an accusation. And it usually works: most people will apologise rather than sit in it, which is the reassurance they came for.
    Why it fails
    What they wanted was to be wanted. What they get is somebody managing them. Reassurance is worthless the moment it arrives, because it was extracted rather than given — so the fear comes back larger and needs a bigger charge next time. And the real grievance never gets said, so it never gets fixed.
    What Scripture puts in its place
    Paul asks who knows a person's thoughts except the spirit of that person (1 Corinthians 2:11). He is making a point about God, but the thing he assumes on his way past is the thing you need: another person's interior is genuinely shut to you. Not hard to read. Shut.

    Which cuts both ways, and that is why it is the right text rather than a convenient one. They cannot tell you what you have decided. And you cannot tell them what they are really doing. Neither of you has been in there. So put the conversation where you can both stand: on what happened, which is checkable, and which you were both present for.

    Taking it off your own face

    This one does not feel like guessing. It feels like knowing somebody well — and often you do, and your reading is right four times in five. That is exactly what makes it hard to catch: it is built out of real attention, and the attention is a virtue.

    The narrow question: the last time you knew why somebody was being off with you, did you say the why out loud as though it were a fact — and did you ever find out? Mostly we never find out, so the theory is never tested, never disproved, and hardens into a thing you know about that person.

    The repair is one small surgery on the sentence. Keep what you saw, cut what you concluded, and leave the gap for them:

    Report what you saw, ask what it means, and do not fill the gap.

    Where it turns up

    This one climbs. At green it is fear with a bad strategy attached. Higher up the same sentence arrives with a track record behind it, or alongside something out of bounds — and then you are no longer looking at somebody frightened. The response barely changes, which is worth noticing early: refusing to argue about interiors works whoever is asking.
