    A Wing of the Kingdom

    The Dojo

    The practice yard, where the kingdom rehearses the conversations it is bad at — slowly, out loud, and with somebody laughing in the background.

    You have stepped into the practice yard. Somebody is being told to run it again. Old Ambrose is on the low wall with a jar of the dark honey, which is generally a sign.

    This is a yard, not an infirmary. You come here in the weeks when nothing is happening, so that you have already said the hard sentence forty times before the afternoon it matters. If you have arrived in the middle of something, you are welcome and it will still help — but the reason a yard is any use is that nothing here costs anybody anything.

    Nothing that happens here happened. Bruno has never spoken to anybody the way he speaks in the green belt drills and would be appalled to be told he had. He will put on whatever you need him to, badly, and enjoy it more than is decent, because that is what a sparring partner is for.

    Each drill gives you a scene and one sentence that lands on you. You choose what you would actually say — then you find out what it caused, three weeks later, with the consequence in it. Ambrose says what he thinks, and only afterwards does he put a name to what you did. The names are held back on purpose: a labelled answer gets picked because it is labelled, and teaches nothing but a script.

    Choose by your own weak side

    The list below is not a field guide to other people. Read it that way and you will go hunting for the one that fits somebody you are cross with, and you will have learned nothing except a new thing to accuse them of.

    Read it the other way round, and notice that there are two ways in, because there are two ways a mask gets you.

    If it is worn at you Which sentence, said to you on a Tuesday, would have you agreeing to something untrue by Tuesday evening — or saying something you would want back?

    If you reach for it And which of them is yours? Which one comes to hand when you are cornered, or tired, or in the wrong and not ready to be?

    Both are worth training and they train differently, which is why every case has two drills: one where it is done to you, and one where you are wearing it. Most people find their two are not the same mask.

    How the belts work → — five minutes, and worth it before you start.

    • The Helper “I was protecting you.” If it is worn at you — you cannot refuse a kindness without feeling ungrateful. If you reach for it — you step in before you ask, because watching somebody struggle is unbearable to you. White belt ready
    • The Mind-Reader “I know how you really feel about me.” If it is worn at you — you feel you have to prove what you were really thinking. If you reach for it — you say why somebody is being off with you as though it were a fact you had checked. Green and brown belts ready
    • The Weather-Keeper “I should have known how that would land.” If it is worn at you — a heavy mood arrives in the room and becomes your job without anybody asking. If you reach for it — you cannot sit down while somebody nearby is unhappy, whatever the cause of it. White and yellow belts ready
    • The Long Winter “You know what kind of year I've had.” If it is worn at you — somebody's real hardship arrives whenever accountability does, and you cannot hold both. If you reach for it — you have spent a true, terrible year to get out of a small thing you did not want to do. Green belt ready
    • The Turn “I can't believe you'd accuse me of that.” If it is worn at you — you end up apologising and cannot reconstruct how. If you reach for it — when a complaint lands on you, your first move is to say how it made you feel. No face on it yet
    • The Peacemaker “I was being kind.” If it is worn at you — somebody's warmth leaves you holding a cost nobody named. If you reach for it — you say yes fast to keep a face from falling, and call it kindness. No face on it yet
    • The Softener “No, honestly, it's alright.” If it is worn at you — you can never tell whether they meant it, so you stop asking. If you reach for it — you say it is fine when it is not, and resent it a fortnight later. No face on it yet
    • The Rulebook “I'm only following the rule.” If it is worn at you — you go quiet the moment somebody is technically correct. If you reach for it — you let the rule say the no you did not want to say in your own voice. No face on it yet
    • The Expert “I'm the one who knows about this.” If it is worn at you — you stop arguing because they know more than you do. If you reach for it — you answer with your standing instead of with the question. No face on it yet
    • The Joke “I was only teasing.” If it is worn at you — you laugh along and feel the sting an hour later. If you reach for it — you land the true thing wrapped up, so you can take it back if it goes badly. No face on it yet
    • The Ledger “After everything I've done for you.” If it is worn at you — you pay debts you never agreed to. If you reach for it — you find yourself totalling up what you have given, on the way to asking for something. No face on it yet
    • The Standard “I'm just honest. I have high standards.” If it is worn at you — you take somebody's sharpness as the price of getting the truth out of them. If you reach for it — you call your own sharpness honesty, and the shortfall in warmth gets renamed a virtue. No face on it yet

    What a yard cannot do

    Worth saying plainly, and saying it here rather than at the end where it would sound like a disclaimer.

    The drills can put a better sentence in your mouth. They cannot make you somebody who wants to hear the answer. A person can learn every response on this site and use all of them to win arguments more efficiently, and be further from where they were trying to get than when they started. Technique will make you better at conversations. It has never once made anybody good.

    So every case ends with a prayer rather than a summary, and the prayer is not a request to be better at this. It confesses the thing under the mask and asks God for the disposition, which is the part no amount of practice reaches. Growth is His to give — one plants, another waters, and neither of them is anything.

    And if the difficulty is not the sentences at all — if it is that being disliked for ten minutes is more than you can currently spend — then this yard is the wrong tool for now, and Truth in Love, in Keep Your Heart, is the right one. It is written for exactly that and says so in its first line.

    Five of these are worn in the stories by people you like. That is not an accident and it is not a criticism of them. It is the whole argument for a practice yard: if masks only ever turned up on villains, nobody would need to learn them.
