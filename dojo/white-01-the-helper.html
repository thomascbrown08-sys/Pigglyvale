    The Dojo · White Belt · Case One

    The Helper

    Somebody did a hard thing for you, kindly, without asking. Nothing was aimed at you. Something was taken anyway.

    The practice yard, after the tables are cleared. Thomas has agreed to wear this one on the grounds that it is his and he ought to know it best. Bruno has been told he may not laugh. Bruno is laughing.

    The drill

    You have a difficult conversation coming. A stallholder is three weeks behind on his account and you are the one who has to say so. You have been working up to it since Tuesday, badly, and you have rehearsed the first sentence four times.

    You arrive at Marketrow and it is done. Somebody has already spoken to him. Kindly, and well, and the account will be settled by Friday.

    He is pleased. He is pleased for you.

    What he says

    “Don't thank me. I could see you were dreading it.”

    Everything about that is true. You were dreading it. He could see it. He meant it entirely and it cost him an awkward quarter of an hour he did not have to spend.

    And something has been taken, and you cannot say what, and the not being able to say what is the whole difficulty of this belt.

    The mask

    “I was protecting you.”

    What it protects underneath
    Not you. Him. Watching somebody you love struggle is genuinely painful, and standing there useless while it happens is worse. The mask lifts both at once.
    What it buys
    Real goods, and they should be said plainly. The hard thing is done. You are spared an afternoon of dread. He gets to be useful to somebody he loves, which is one of the better feelings available to a person.
    Why it fails
    It answers a need he has and charges it to you. And the thing it takes is not the errand — it is the evidence. You were about to find out you could do it. Now you will not, and next time the dread will be the same size or bigger, because nothing happened to shrink it.
    What Scripture puts in its place
    Paul says bear one another's burdens and then, four verses later, that each will have to bear his own load — which looks like a contradiction until you notice he has used two different words. The first is a crushing weight, more than one person can carry. The second is a man's own pack, the ordinary size of thing he is meant to walk under. The command is to get under what would crush somebody. It is not to carry their pack, and doing it anyway is not obedience to the first verse but disobedience to the second. Lifting a burden is love. Lifting a load says you could not have. — Galatians 6:2, 6:5

    Three roads and a fourth

    Two ditches, and they look like opposites. They are not. Both of them are ways of protecting yourself — one from the awkwardness, one from the smallness. Only the road costs you something, which is how you know which one it is.

    Left — caving

    “Oh — thank you. That was so kind of you.”

    Buys — a pleasant afternoon. He stays happy. Nobody is awkward. And it is not even a lie: it was kind.

    Costs — you have just told him it worked, so he will do it again, and the next thing he takes will be bigger. You have also agreed to his account of you: that you were dreading it and needed rescuing. That account is now yours too.

    Right — lashing

    “I didn't ask you to do that.”

    Buys — it is true, it is immediate, and the boundary is out of your mouth instead of rotting in your chest for a fortnight.

    Costs — it lands as ingratitude on somebody who spent his afternoon on you, so the next ten minutes are about your tone rather than his action. It aims at him rather than at the thing he did. And it tells him nothing about what to do differently, which means you will be here again.

    The road

    “That was kind, and I'd rather you hadn't. I was working up to it, and I needed to be the one who said it. Will you ask me first next time?”

    Buys — the kindness is named as real, because it was, and saying so is not a concession. The cost is named without a verdict attached. And it ends in a request, which leaves him somewhere to go: there is a version of him that gets this right, and you have just described it.

    Costs — a distinctly uncomfortable ten seconds, and the risk that he is hurt anyway. He might be. That is not evidence you said it wrong.

    Further along the same road

    “That was kind, and I'd rather you hadn't. I've been dreading it for three weeks and I was ashamed of that, which is why I never said anything about it. But I needed to be the one who said it to him. Will you ask me next time — even if I look like I'm drowning?”

    Buys — everything above, and one thing more. He was reaching for something real: he could see you were struggling and he wanted in. The short version shuts the door he was knocking on. This one opens it and puts him on the other side of it, where he can actually be of use.

    Costs — you have to say the shameful part out loud, to the person who already saw it. That is the price, and it is why this version is further along the road rather than merely a nicer way of saying the same thing.

    Why the last one is best

    Because it is not the average of the two ditches.

    That is the mistake nearly everybody makes with a middle road — they assume it means somewhat firm and somewhat nice, a bit of each ditch mixed until it is drinkable. It is not on that line at all. Caving and lashing are both about you: one buys you out of the awkwardness, the other buys you out of feeling small. They are the same move in different clothes.

    The road is the one that costs you something for his sake. That is what makes it the road, and it is why the better version is the harder one rather than the smoother one. If a response ever gets easier as it gets better, check it again. Something has gone wrong.

    The mask on yourself

    This one is worth being careful about, because almost nobody wearing it thinks they are. It does not feel like taking. It feels like love, and the reason it feels like love is that most of it is love. That is what makes it a mask rather than a lie.

    So the question is not do I help too much. That question has no answer and everybody answers it wrong. The question is narrower and it has a real answer:

    When you stepped in — did you ask her first? And if you didn't, what were you afraid would happen if you had?

    Sit with the second half. The honest answer is usually she would have said no, and then I would have had to stand there and watch. Which means the help was not only for her. Some of it was for you, and the asking got skipped because asking risked an answer you did not want.

    That is not a monstrous thing to discover about yourself. It is a very ordinary one. But it does mean the fix is not helping less. It is one sentence, before:

    “Would you like me to, or would you rather do it yourself?”

    And if you have already done it — and you will, because everyone does — then the mask comes off out loud, with the parts named:

    “I did that without asking you. I did it because watching you struggle is hard for me, and that is my reason and it is not an excuse. What would you like me to do now?”

    Which is roughly what Thomas said in Episode One, standing in the doorway of the Kitchen Royal, after a four-year-old with a basket of eggs asked him the question he had never once thought to ask himself.

    Practise it

    1. Say the road version out loud, twice, until it stops sounding rude. It is not rude. It only feels that way because the first half is a compliment and your mouth expects the second half to be one too.
    2. Think of the last time somebody helped you in a way that left you smaller. What would you have wanted them to do instead? Now say that sentence as though they were in the room.
    3. The harder one. Think of the last time you did it to somebody. Did you ask?
