    Episode Three

    Quantity Not Stated

    In which an agreement is reached in about nine seconds by two people who have known each other thirty years, and neither of them is lying about what it was.

    The peppers came in during the third week of cold nights, which in Pigglyvale is the week when everybody's eyes water on the way past Marketrow and nobody minds.

    Auntie Yolanda Plum had nine bushels of them stacked three deep behind her stall and the face of a woman standing in front of a problem she had invented herself. Carolyn stopped at the top of the row, because you could smell it from there: the particular smell of a great deal of something that will not keep.

    “Nine bushels.”

    “Eleven, if Gus brings his,” said Auntie Yolanda. “And the cold coming Thursday.”

    “Then they want putting up.”

    “They want putting up together.” The goose said it with both wings out, which is how she says most things. “You and me and a great deal of vinegar. Like we used to.”

    “Of course,” said Carolyn.

    The whole thing took nine seconds and neither of them broke stride. Both of them walked away from it happy. And it is worth saying now, plainly, before anything else happens, that they had just agreed to two completely different things, and that not one word of what either of them said was untrue.

    The first cart arrived on the Tuesday, and Thomas attempted the side door with it, on the theory that a cart changes the geometry. It does not.

    By three the worktable had disappeared under baskets, and Pim was seeding with a paring knife held in both paws and asking every eleven minutes whether the vinegar wanted stirring.

    “The other four carts come Thursday,” said Auntie Yolanda, from inside a basket.

    Carolyn's hands did not stop moving. Her head did the sum without being asked to, the way it always does around food: eleven bushels, call it sixty jars, six hours of boiling and a night of cooling and a morning of sealing, against the two batches this kitchen holds at once. Which is not a fortnight of afternoons. It is a fortnight of nights.

    Four seconds. She had it completely.

    “Right,” she said.

    Miss Delphine Quill came in at four with the register under her arm and a single loose sheet on top of it.

    “A person not on the household roll is using the Kitchen Royal.”

    “It is Auntie Yolanda, Delphine.”

    “It is.” Miss Quill set the sheet down on the one clear inch of table. “This is the new rule. You said you would sign whatever I brought you.”

    It was four lines long. Carolyn read it standing up, with a pepper in each hand.

    What is being made.
    How much of it.
    Who it is for.
    When it stops.

    “That is the whole rule?”

    “That is the whole rule. The seven days' notice is gone; it never protected anybody. Both parties fill it in together, at the start, out loud.” She squared the sheet to the edge of the table. “It takes four minutes.”

    “Delphine,” said Auntie Yolanda from inside the basket, “it is peppers.”

    “Leave it with me,” said Carolyn, warmly, and meant it warmly, and put the sheet on the dresser under the salt jar. “I will sign whatever you have written the minute we have this lot off the road. You know that.”

    Miss Quill wrote something in the register. It took her four words and no time at all, and she said nothing about it, and went back to the records room, where the window is always open because of the ink.

    Stand in Auntie Yolanda's shoes a minute, because from inside them none of this is confusing. She asked, properly, in the open, on the row — and her friend of thirty years said of course without a half-second's hesitation, which between the two of them has always meant one thing: yes, all of it, obviously, do not insult me by asking twice. She was not guessing. She had three decades of evidence and every scrap of it pointed the same way.

    So on the Wednesday she told the row there would be a jar for every house before the cold, and said it the way you say a thing you are proud of. Gus Thornapple heard it, thought that is a great many jars, and put it away to mention later.

    And in the Kitchen Royal her friend of thirty years stood at a table she could no longer see the top of, doing arithmetic she had already done four times and getting the same answer, and saying nothing, because saying it would have made the kitchen awkward for about an hour.

    An hour is nothing. That was the reasoning, and it is not a stupid one. She simply preferred a fortnight of tired to an hour of awkward, the way she has preferred it her whole life, and she has never once written that trade down where she could look at it.

    Thomas noticed on the Friday, and what he noticed was entirely to his credit: the two of them described the same undertaking twice in one afternoon and the descriptions did not match. Auntie Yolanda said the crop. Carolyn said a few good batches. Nobody blinked but him.

    So he went away and did what he believes he is for. He drew up a fair and orderly summary of what had been agreed, headed A MEMORANDUM OF THE PEPPER UNDERTAKING, and because he cannot carry two of anything he had Pim run one copy to the kitchen and one to the stall. It was clear, it was generous to both parties, and it was, from top to bottom, what he would have agreed. He had not been on the road on the Monday. Where the two accounts disagreed he had filled the gap with a reasonable duck.

    By the second Wednesday the kitchen smelled permanently of hot vinegar and there were thirty-one jars on the dresser, which is a great many jars unless somebody has promised sixty.

    Carolyn had been up three nights out of five and had said nothing about any of them. And Auntie Yolanda, who loves her, had begun improving the kitchen: the salt and sugar swapped round at last, the big pot moved where a sensible person would keep it, and the whole rack of wooden spoons taken down, wiped and rehung.

    Pim came in on the Thursday and stood in front of the rack a while. Every spoon was hanging handle inward. She took down the little one, the one she is allowed, turned it round, hung it back the way she was taught, and did not touch the others, because they were not hers to turn. Carolyn saw her do it, from the hearth, with both hands in a pot — and was too tired to say anything, and noticed that she was too tired, and filed it beside the other thing she was not saying. That is how these stack. One at a time, each perfectly reasonable on its own.

    What broke it was three baskets and a doorway.

    Thursday, mid-morning: Beatrix Hollyhock at the kitchen door with a basket and two of the Marketrow wives behind her with baskets, all three cheerful, all three come for their jars, because Auntie Yolanda had said. Beatrix's big oven has been going a fortnight now, Gus having said three weeks and it having been ten, which nobody has mentioned to Gus.

    “There are thirty-one,” said Carolyn, in the doorway.

    “There are two hundred souls,” said Beatrix, doing the sum out loud and then hearing herself do it and going quiet.

    Auntie Yolanda came up the path with the fifth cart and into the middle of it.

    “We agreed the crop.”

    “We agreed three afternoons,” said Carolyn. “You and me and a pot. That is what I said yes to on the road.”

    “I said together. I said it in front of the whole row—”

    “You said it about nine bushels of peppers that were already behind your stall.”

    “And you said of course!”

    Thomas, who had been standing there some moments with his crest feathers rising, produced his memorandum and read out what had been agreed, in the interests of clarity.

    Both women turned and looked at him, and neither of them recognised a word of it.

    “Where did you get that?”

    “I —” said Thomas. His spectacles came down his bill and stayed there. “I composed it.”

    Nobody said anything. And into that Miss Quill, who had come up the path behind the cart and waited with the patience of a woman who is never in a hurry because she is always on time, opened the register at the Tuesday of the week before and turned it round on the step.

    Four words, in her small upright hand.

    Peppers. Quantity not stated.

    It stopped being funny somewhere around the second word.

    They sent everyone home with a jar between them and shut the door.

    Carolyn sat down on the end of the bench, which put her below Auntie Yolanda rather than across from her, and which she has learned is the only honest way to start one of these.

    “I knew on the second afternoon,” she said. “Not Thursday. The second afternoon. You said four more carts and I did the arithmetic standing there and I got it right — a fortnight of nights, not three afternoons — and I said right, and I said nothing else. Twelve days ago.”

    “Carolyn—”

    “Let me finish it, Auntie, because the next part is the part I am actually sorry for. I did not keep quiet because I was being kind to you. I kept quiet because saying it would have made this kitchen awkward for about an hour, and I would rather be tired for a fortnight than awkward for an hour. That is not generosity. It is comfort, and I have been calling it the other thing for years.” She looked up. “And because I said nothing, you stood on the row and promised sixty jars in good faith, and I let you find that out in a doorway in front of Beatrix Hollyhock. So here is what I will do instead. When I have counted a thing and the count comes out wrong, I will say the number out loud on the day I get it. To your face, on the day. Even when it makes the room awkward. Especially then.”

    Auntie Yolanda's beak worked for a while.

    “You said of course,” she said at last. “And I heard the rest of it. I heard the whole rest of it, Carolyn, the crop and the jars and the two of us at that table till midnight, and I heard it so clearly that I would have sworn in front of anybody that you said it. You never said one word of it.”

    She sat down on the step, heavily, and it is a long way down for a goose.

    “I fill it in,” she said. “When somebody does not say a thing, I put in what I would have said, and then I go about believing I was told. I have done that for thirty years and I have never once in my life called it making things up.”

    “Auntie.”

    “No. Say the rest of it, because you will not and I will. I told the row a thing you had not agreed to, and I told it about you, and I told it because it made me proud to be your friend in front of people. That is mine. I will go back up the row this afternoon and take it off them one by one, and I will not do it by saying there was a misunderstanding, because there was not. I said it and it was not true.”

    “That is going to be a horrible walk. Would you like company on it?”

    “No,” said Auntie Yolanda. “Thank you. It does not work if you come.”

    She was gone two hours, and came back with one jar a household had handed back to her under the circumstances, and set it on the table between them like a receipt.

    They finished on the Sunday, thirty-one jars becoming forty-four, which is not sixty and was never going to be. It went down the Long Table that evening in whatever each household had brought — jam jars, a jug, in one case a boot.

    Beatrix came with bread out of her own oven for the first time since the summer. Marisol Vega brought pan sobao anyway, on the grounds that two breads has never once been a problem. Bruno arrived towing the cart, which now said MARROW & DAUGHTERS, PURVEYORS, and when asked purveyors of what said he would know by Tuesday.

    The sheet came out from under the salt jar somewhere around the second round of bread. Miss Quill read the four lines aloud, one at a time, waiting after each, and both women answered each one out loud in front of two hundred people. It was the least dramatic thing that has ever happened in that courtyard, and it took four minutes.

    “Boring,” said Auntie Yolanda.

    “Yes,” said Miss Quill, ruling a line under it. “That is what it is for.”

    Gus Thornapple, who had been told at some length by three separate people that he had been right, was so pleased he had to go and stand behind a wall.

    After the Washing-Up

    The Kitchen Royal, late, and smelling of vinegar in a way it will for a month. Carolyn is turning the spoons on the rack the right way round, one at a time. Thomas has the book tucked under one wing.

    THOMAS. I have worked out what went wrong, and I should like to say it before I lose my nerve about the second half.

    CAROLYN. Go on.

    THOMAS. Two people made an agreement and neither of them described it. Each one carried away the version that suited her, and the fault lies in the carrying. (the wing tip splaying into its rough point) One ought not to fill in a gap somebody has left.

    CAROLYN. That is nearly it.

    THOMAS. (the feathers on the back of his head going up) Nearly.

    CAROLYN. You have got the emphasis on the wrong one. Auntie Yolanda filled in a gap, yes. But somebody has to leave a gap before anybody can fill it, and I left it. I have been leaving them for thirty years, because a gap is very comfortable to stand in. Nobody can be disappointed in a gap.

    THOMAS. (a pause; his spectacles sliding down his bill) Ah.

    CAROLYN. So: everything in writing between the two of us, from now on. Delphine's four lines, every time, for anything bigger than a Tuesday.

    THOMAS. And then what happens?

    CAROLYN. And then we both know what we agreed.

    THOMAS. Do we? Majesty, put that sheet in front of Auntie Yolanda Plum. What does she do with it?

    CAROLYN. (stopping with a spoon halfway to its hook) …She signs it.

    THOMAS. Without reading it.

    CAROLYN. Without reading a word of it, because to that woman reading it in front of me would be an accusation. She would sign it fast, to show me she trusted me, and she would sign it faster the more it mattered. And we would be exactly where we started with a drawer full of paper.

    THOMAS. Then what did work? Because something did.

    CAROLYN. It was not the paper. It was Delphine standing there making us say the four things at each other. The writing is only what is left over afterwards. The whole of the machine is the four minutes where you have to hear yourself say a number in front of the other person, and find out whether she flinches at it.

    THOMAS. (after a while) You do that with a pot, you know.

    CAROLYN. I do what with a pot?

    THOMAS. You put the garlic in last so it will not scorch. Not because the garlic is burning — because of what it will do to everything that comes after it, four steps down the line, in a pot that is not even hot yet. You did eleven bushels in your head in four seconds and got it right to the jar. You have never once in your life failed to count a pot.

    CAROLYN. Peppers are not people.

    THOMAS. No. But the counting is the same counting, and you already own it, and I am not going to be the one who tells you where else it fits.

    CAROLYN. (hanging the last spoon, handle outward) Hm.

    There is a name in the practice yard for the half of this that belongs to Auntie Yolanda — it is the Mind-Reader, and she is the one wearing it there, which she thinks is very funny.

    Advisor’s Notes

    Thomas the Drake of Snouton, Storykeeper and Master of Lessons

    Before anything else, a debt. Last time I wrote in this book, Old Ambrose came by with a jar of the dark honey and observed that I had not recognised Marisol Vega, I had liked her, and that the two feel identical from the inside while only one of them takes any time. I said something at the time about the discernment proper to my office, which is what a bird says when he is buying himself a fortnight. I have had the fortnight. He was right, I was wrong, and the wrongness was not a slip — it is the way I do it. I arrive at the conclusion and then go back along the road collecting reasons, and I have been calling the collecting judgement. I am not going to promise never to do it again, because I would be doing the very same thing with the promise. What I will do is say out loud how long I have had an opinion when I offer it. Four minutes is a perfectly good length of time in which to like somebody. It is not a length of time in which to vouch for anybody.

    What happened

    Two friends of thirty years agreed on the road to put up the year's peppers together, in about nine seconds. Twelve days later each of them described what had been agreed, in a doorway, in front of three neighbours with baskets, and the two descriptions had almost nothing in common.

    Where the line ran

    Auntie Yolanda Plum was not guessing and it is important to say so. She asked in the open, she was answered without hesitation, and she had three decades of evidence about what that particular answer from that particular friend has always meant. Then she did the thing she has always done, which is to supply the half that was not said, and go about believing she had been told it. She has never had a reason to doubt the practice, because for thirty years it has mostly worked.

    Her Majesty had the correct number on the second afternoon and held on to it for twelve days. Not out of pride, and not to spare anybody — she has told me so herself, and I would not have got there on my own. An hour of awkwardness in her own kitchen was, on the day, more expensive to her than a fortnight of nights. That is a real price and she has been paying it in that currency for a very long while, and she has never once written the trade down where she could look at it.

    And I wrote a memorandum. Nobody asked me for it. I had not been on the road on the Monday and I did not hear the nine seconds, so where the two accounts disagreed I put in what a reasonable and orderly drake would have meant — which is precisely the thing Auntie Yolanda did, in a nicer hand, on better paper, by a Storykeeper who had just spent a fortnight being told about it. I did notice this one myself, which is new, and I noticed it only after I had handed it to both of them, which is not. I would rather record it in that order than pretend the order was better.

    Nobody lied. That is the thing I keep coming back to. There was not one untrue sentence in the whole fortnight, and it still cost a good woman a horrible walk up Marketrow taking a promise back off her neighbours one at a time.

    What Scripture says, and how we got there

    Our Lord is walking with great crowds and He turns round and says something that thins them out considerably: this will cost you everything, and you had better know that before you set off. Then, to make the point, He reaches for the most ordinary example He can find.

    For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it? — Luke 14:28, ESV

    Notice the shape of the sentence before the sense of it. Which of you does not? It is not a command. It is not even advice. It is put as a rhetorical question, the sort you ask when the answer is so obvious that nobody in the crowd will raise a hand — because everybody there had watched somebody build something, and everybody there knew that you sit down first. Our Lord can borrow the practice to illustrate something enormous precisely because the practice itself was not in dispute.

    Which turns the usual reading inside out. We tend to hear count the cost as a caution, a cold shower poured on somebody's enthusiasm. In the passage it is nothing of the kind. It is the ordinary thing. The strange act, the one the crowd would have laughed at, is not counting — and the man who did not count is not described as an optimist. He is described as a man with half a tower standing in a field where everybody can see it.

    Now the part that reaches this kitchen, and it comes out of the next illustration rather than the first. Our Lord immediately gives a second one: a king going to war who sits down and reckons whether ten thousand can meet twenty thousand, and if they cannot, he sends a delegation and asks for terms. He does not sit in a tent and conclude privately. The counting has a second party in it, and the counting is said. Which is exactly the piece the peppers turned on. A tower with one builder needs one count. A tower with two builders needs two counts, said out loud at each other, or each of them is quietly costing a different tower and both of them are perfectly happy until the stone runs out.

    Both of these women sat down and counted. Neither of them was careless. They counted different towers.

    I should be honest about a borrowing, because I have made a small use of a very large passage. Luke 14 is not about pepper sauce. It is about the cost of following Christ, and it says so plainly four verses later, and it is a harder saying than anything in this episode. I have taken the illustration and left the argument it was serving. I think that is fair, because the illustration only works if the everyday prudence in it is real — you cannot grant the passage its force and then deny the small point it is standing on. But it is a borrowing, and I would rather say so than let it pass as exposition.

    There is one thing left, and it is what makes the four minutes so hard between people who love each other. Being asked to be specific feels like being told you are not trusted. Auntie Yolanda felt it in my kitchen — it is peppers — and she was not being unreasonable; between friends, a demand for particulars really can be an insult, and we have all met the person who uses precision as a way of keeping a foot on somebody. But the two are told apart easily enough by asking what the precision is for. A count made to protect yourself from the other person is one thing. A count made so that the two of you are building the same tower is the opposite thing, and it is not doubt about the project. It is respect for it. The insult was never in the asking. It is in a promise made on somebody else's behalf and discovered in a doorway.

    Try it this week

    Take Miss Quill's four lines to the next thing you agree to with somebody you are fond of — and do not write them down first. Say them.

    “Before we start: what are we making, how much of it, who is it for, and when does it stop?”

    Then the second half, which is the half that does the work, because it is the one that catches you rather than them:

    “Say it back to me the way you heard it.”

    If the two of you have heard the same thing, this will be boring and will take four minutes and you will feel slightly silly. Boring is the good outcome. Boring is what you are paying for. And if it is not boring, you have just found the difference on a Tuesday, in a kitchen, over nothing — instead of on a Thursday in a doorway with three neighbours holding baskets.

    The Movement Card — Episode Three

    Two entries this month, and one of them is a dot. Marisol Vega has been coming to the Kitchen Royal twice a week since the autumn, on time, without anybody having to think about her — which is the whole of how a person moves one ring, and why it takes a season and not a fortnight. Auntie Yolanda does not move at all. She has had her place at the Long Table for thirty years and nothing that happened this month put a mark on it. What changed was a key: the Kitchen Royal now runs on four written lines, read out loud before anybody starts. A seat and a key are different things, and this is what it looks like when only one of them is adjusted.

    From My Toolbox

    The half you did not say, said back to you

    There is a belief that grows on long friendships the way rust grows on a good pan, and it sounds like a compliment: we have known each other so long that we should not have to spell things out. Underneath it is a quiet swap. Being close stops meaning I know this person well and starts meaning I no longer have to check. And once checking has become optional, it starts to feel like an accusation — so the closer two people are, the less either of them says.

    Watch where it shows in this episode. Auntie Yolanda hears of course and hears a whole paragraph after it, and she is not being careless: she has thirty years of evidence and she is reading it the way anybody would. What she has lost is not the ability to listen. It is the habit of holding a gap open as a gap — of letting a thing somebody did not say stay unsaid, and uncomfortable, and available to be asked about. She fills it in the way water fills a shape, and by the time it is full she can no longer tell which part of it she was given and which part she poured. “I have never once in my life called it making things up.”

    Here is the cost, and notice that it lands on the friendship rather than on her. She stops being able to be surprised by the person she loves most. Somebody she has known thirty years cannot get news to her any more, because every gap in the news gets filled with the version she already had. And the moment the fill turns out to be wrong, it is not a small correction — it is a promise made on somebody's behalf and taken back one household at a time.

    The turn worth having is not trust her less. It is that asking is not the opposite of trusting, and the four minutes are not a test. A love that has to guess is doing considerably more work than a love that asks, and getting a worse result for the effort. The specificity is not suspicion wearing a coat. It is what you do when the other person's actual answer matters more to you than the one you had ready.

    Held as a picture and said to be one: filling in the gap is a way of describing a habit, not a diagnosis of anybody, and the useful version of it stays pointed at your own guessing rather than at somebody else's.

    Talk it over

    1. Carolyn had the right number on the second afternoon and did not say it for twelve days. What did the silence buy her — and was it worth the price, on the second afternoon, before she knew how it ended?
    2. Thomas wrote out what he thought had been agreed, in good faith, having not been there. When has somebody settled a thing between you and another person by supplying the middle of it? Was it a help?
    3. Auntie Yolanda says she has been filling in the unsaid half for thirty years and has never called it making things up. Where is the line between knowing somebody well and deciding what they meant? (I have looked for it all week and I cannot find one that holds. I suspect the honest answer is that there is no line, only the asking.)

    Pique

    From the Kitchen Royal and Auntie Yolanda Plum's stall, as made over a fortnight that need only have taken three afternoons.

    • 20–30 small hot peppers (ají caballero, or bird's eye, or whatever the row has), stems off
    • 1 head of garlic, peeled and lightly crushed
    • 1 small onion, quartered
    • A large handful of fresh oregano or cilantro, stalks and all
    • 1 teaspoon black peppercorns
    • 2 teaspoons salt
    • 1 tablespoon honey, or a thick slice of pineapple with the skin on
    • 750ml white or cane vinegar

    Pack the peppers, garlic, onion, herbs and peppercorns into a clean jar or bottle, loosely enough that the vinegar can get everywhere. Warm the vinegar with the salt and the honey until the salt has gone — not boiling, just hot — and pour it over. Leave an inch at the top.

    Seal it and put it somewhere you will walk past. It is drinkable in three days, good in a week, and properly itself at a month. The pineapple, if you use it, will make it faintly sweet and slightly fizzy in the first fortnight; this is correct and not a fault.

    Top the bottle up with vinegar as you use it, and it will keep going for a year. Two shakes on rice and beans, on stewed chicken, on a fried egg, on anything that has gone a little quiet. And write on the label how many jars you made, because somebody will ask.
