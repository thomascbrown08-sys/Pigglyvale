    Episode Two

    The Column Nobody Read

    In which a stranger arrives with her own oven in a week the kingdom has no oven, and everybody is asked to be delighted about it.

    Fair Week ended on a Sunday, and by Monday the kingdom had the particular flatness of a place that has been on its best behaviour for eleven days and would now like to lie down.

    Beatrix Hollyhock’s big oven was still cracked up the back wall. Gus Thornapple had said three weeks in the voice he keeps for five.

    So when a stranger came up the one good road on Tuesday towing a barrel-shaped clay oven on a handcart — domed, sooted, still faintly warm from wherever she had slept — Pigglyvale did not so much greet her as inhale.

    “Vega,” she said, to nobody in particular, setting the handles down on the cobbles. “Marisol. I bake.”

    She was tall and rangy and about fifty, silver at her temples under an indigo headwrap, with the forearms of somebody who has been doing one thing for a very long time. By ten she had the fire drawing. By half past there were eleven loaves of pan sobao on a board, split and shining with lard, and the smell went up Marketrow like a rumour.

    Thomas came down at eleven to record her arrival, on the grounds that a baker arriving in a week without a baker was a matter of state. He came back at ten past.

    “Majesty,” he said, arranging himself in the kitchen doorway, which he filled the way a cork fills a bottle. “She is one of us.”

    “You met her four minutes ago.”

    “I met her sufficiently.” The wing tip came out and splayed into its rough point, which is the nearest Thomas has to a raised finger. “One recognises the sort who has been welcomed everywhere because she earned it everywhere.”

    Carolyn, elbow-deep in a bowl of soaked pigeon peas, thought this was funny at the time.

    By Thursday Marisol Vega was at the long scrubbed worktable with her sleeves rolled and her own scraper in her hand, and Carolyn had given her the good hearth, the cold larder, and the key that hangs by the door.

    It was not a hard decision, and that is worth saying plainly. Beatrix had a fair-week backlog and no oven; Marisol had an oven and forty years of bread; the kingdom ate. Anyone would have done it. Carolyn simply did it faster, and enjoyed it more, and stood in her kitchen at the end of the first day laughing at a story about a burnt wedding cake six ridges over, and thought: oh, good. Somebody easy.

    Miss Delphine Quill arrived at four with the register under her arm and her pencil already out. “A person not on the household roll is using the Kitchen Royal,” she said. “Under whose authority.”

    “Mine, Delphine.”

    “Very well.” She wrote it down and said nothing else, because Miss Quill has never in her life said a thing that did not need saying.

    The quarterly came next morning and took forty minutes, as it does. Carolyn sat at the end of the worktable with a mug going cold and initialled her way down the columns: stores in, stores out, road and repairs, lamp oil, the fair account, the levy nobody pays because there is nothing to levy.

    And then the seventh column, which has no heading, and which Carolyn has been putting her initials beside for twenty years without once asking what it is.

    The bread order ran late, and by eleven the Kitchen Royal looked as though a small weather event had happened in it. Marisol had her boots off and her feet on the fender, doing an impression of a man in Snouton who once tried to pay her in ducks, and Carolyn laughed until she had to put a hand on the table.

    “Oh,” she said, when she had it back. “That is the first time I have laughed properly in a fortnight.”

    “Fair Week.”

    “Fair Week, and the quarterly, and—” She stopped, and then did not stop, because it was late and she was tired and here at last was somebody who wanted nothing from her. “Some days I would give anything for one person here who didn’t need me to be delighted about their columns.”

    It was not a cruel sentence, and it was not an untrue one. It was the unfinished version of herself, said out loud at eleven at night to somebody she had known since Tuesday.

    Marisol laughed, and topped up the coffee, and thought no more about it at all.

    Two days later she told it at Marketrow.

    She told it warmly. That has to be understood or the whole thing goes wrong. She was at Gus Thornapple’s barrels with half the row around her, being funny about the Princess the way you are funny about somebody you have decided to love, and doing what anybody does in a new place: proving she had been let in.

    “— and she says to me, eleven at night, she says, some days I would give anything for one person here who didn’t need me to be delighted about their columns—”

    Marketrow laughed, because it was funny.

    Miss Quill was four feet away, on the other side of the barrels, with a basket of allspice and a list.

    Gus saw her hear it. Gus sees everything nine seconds before anybody else and mentions it a week late, and this was no exception; he watched her turn and go up the row very straight and very fast, thought well, that is not good, and went back to his barrels.

    Nothing happened for a day. Then everything did, and all of it was legal.

    On Monday the lamp oil for the Long Table lanterns, which for twenty years had gone out of the common store on the Princess’s word, required a signed docket in advance. On Tuesday the flour drawn against the fair account came back with a note explaining, correctly, that the account had closed on the last Sunday of Fair Week. On Wednesday Her Majesty was informed in writing that a person not on the household roll might use the Kitchen Royal only under a countersigned grant, and that a grant required seven days’ notice.

    Marisol’s oven went cold that afternoon. Beatrix’s was still cracked. On Thursday there was no bread in Pigglyvale at all, for the first time in anybody’s memory, and two hundred souls discovered they had opinions about bread.

    Every one of those decisions was correct. That is what nobody could get hold of. No rule bent, no spite written down, nothing to point at and call unfair. Miss Quill had simply stopped doing the thing she had done unasked for twenty years, which was to quietly let her Princess through.

    Carolyn went to the records room on Thursday with three weeks of Fair Week behind her and no sleep in her, and she was sharp.

    “Seven days.”

    “Seven days, Majesty.”

    “Delphine, there are people who cannot get a loaf.”

    “There are.” Miss Quill did not look up. The pencil went on down the page, very even. “It is a poor rule. I have said so twice, in writing, in the years you have been signing it.”

    “Then bend it. You have bent it for me every week of my life, and now, on the one week the whole kingdom needs—” and she heard her own voice going up and did not stop it, which is the part she thought about afterwards. “What is this for?”

    Miss Quill set the pencil down and squared it to the edge of the page.

    “You will have your grant on Wednesday,” she said. “I am sure you can be delighted about it by then.”

    The clock in the records room is loud. Carolyn had never noticed that before.

    She stood there and did the arithmetic, and it took four seconds. She heard it. Somewhere I did not say it — which means I said it to somebody, and I said it about her, and I have spent ten minutes explaining to a woman I hurt that she is being unreasonable.

    “Delphine.”

    “The office closes at five, Majesty.”

    “Delphine, please. Sit down.”

    It took a while. Miss Quill sat the way a person sits when they are not sure the chair is meant for them: on the front third of it, spines up, both hands flat on her knees.

    “I said something about you to a woman I had known four days,” she said. “I said it in my own kitchen at eleven at night and I meant it, which is worse, not better. I gave a stranger something that belonged to you — twenty years of you, and how tired I am, and the version of me that only comes out late — and then I let you find out at Marketrow, behind a barrel, from somebody who did not know she was carrying it.”

    She did not say I was exhausted. She had it ready, and she left it where it was.

    “It was not a slip. It is the shape of how I have been doing this. I hand things over fast when somebody is easy to be around, and I have been giving you the slow version for two decades and calling it respect. So here is what I will do instead. I will bring the hard part of my week to the person who has actually been carrying it. And if I have not got the patience for your columns on a Thursday, I will say so to you, on Thursday, in words, and we will sort it out between us.”

    Miss Quill looked at the desk for a long moment.

    “Twenty years,” she said, “and nobody has ever asked me to sit down.”

    Three yellow butterflies came in over the window ledge, which is always open in that room because of the ink. One went along the top of the shelf and settled on the spine of a ledger from a year before either of them was in the room, and stayed.

    Neither of them said anything about it.

    “I should like,” said Miss Quill at last, in the voice she uses for reading out the stores, “to record an irregularity.”

    “Delphine—”

    “The docket. The fair account. The grant rule at full length. All three were within the book.” Her spines had gone down flat. “And I decided all three on the Friday afternoon, in the same hour, in advance. That is not keeping the rules. That is using them. I have never done it before, I did it to you, and there is no column for it.”

    She stopped. She was not good at it. She had built the whole thing in her head on the walk over and it had come out flat and in the wrong order, the way a first one does.

    “I am sorry,” she said. “That is the plain sentence. I could not find a better one.”

    “It is the right one,” said Carolyn. “I take it. It is done.”

    Here is what did not happen next.

    Carolyn did not make Miss Quill Keeper of Anything, and did not stand her up at the Long Table to say a warm thing about her in front of two hundred people — the easiest fifteen seconds of the week, and one Miss Quill would have survived roughly the way a hedgehog survives a surprise party.

    And Marisol Vega did not leave. She got her grant on the Wednesday, entirely in order, then looked at the numbers and set the clay oven up at the top of Marketrow instead, three stalls along from Gus. She still comes to the Kitchen Royal twice a week and is still the funniest person in it. She does not know that anything ever happened, which is not the sort of thing you tell somebody who did nothing wrong.

    What happened was the Long Table on the Friday, lit off a docket signed the previous Tuesday. Bruno arrived towing the cart, which now said MARROW & DAUGHTERS in fresh paint, and when asked which daughters, said it was a long game. Thomas took his own small table at the end, on the grounds that he takes up the space of three. There was pan sobao still warm, rice with pigeon peas, and greens with enough pot likker to be worth a second piece of bread.

    Carolyn brought a plate down to the end of the bench where Miss Quill sat with her jacket still buttoned to the throat, and sat down beside her.

    “Delphine. I have a problem and I want your judgement on it, not your ruling. The grant rule is bad and we have both known it for years. Would you write me a better one? Properly — yours, not mine, and I will sign whatever you bring me.”

    Miss Quill was quiet for a second.

    “It will want a month,” she said.

    “Take two.”

    “It will not want two.”

    “No,” said Carolyn. “I did not think it would.” And then, because it had been sitting in her since Friday: “The seventh column. The one with no heading. I have been initialling it since I was nineteen and I have never asked what it is.”

    “No,” agreed Miss Quill. “Nobody has.”

    She had the book with her, because she always has the book with her. She turned it round on the boards, put one finger at the top of the column, and Carolyn read down it in the lantern light.

    It was names. Just names, in Miss Quill’s small upright hand, each with a date beside it, and beside a good many of them a second date further along, and beside a very few of them nothing at all.

    “Who was last at the Long Table, and when,” said Miss Quill. “The second date is when they came back. It is not an official column. I have never billed anyone for it.”

    Carolyn did not say anything for a while.

    At the top of the current page, in the same small hand, was Hollyhock, B., and a date six weeks back, and no second date beside it at all.

    “Her oven,” said Carolyn.

    “Her oven,” said Miss Quill.

    Carolyn stood up and picked up a plate and filled it, and then stood there holding it, because it was a fifteen-minute walk to the bakery in the dark and she had no intention of doing it alone.

    “Come with me,” she said.

    Miss Quill got up, and buttoned a button that was already buttoned, and came.

    After the Washing-Up

    The Kitchen Royal, late. Carolyn is skimming the top off a pot of beans she has no intention of serving to anyone tonight. Thomas has the book tucked under one wing and has been not-saying-something for eleven minutes.

    THOMAS. I have been trying to put a name to what Delphine does.

    CAROLYN. (not looking up from the pot) Try it on me.

    THOMAS. She keeps those books the way a tenant pays rent. Every month, on time, in full, never once late — because she has got it into her head that the rent is what holds the roof up over her. (his wing tip splayed into its rough point)

    CAROLYN. That’s close.

    THOMAS. (the feathers on the back of his head going up) Close.

    CAROLYN. It’s the right picture, turned the wrong way round. She isn’t paying rent so she can stay in the room. She thinks the rent is the only thing she has that anybody wants. Take the ledger off her and she couldn’t tell you one reason a person would keep her.

    THOMAS. (a long pause; his spectacles slid down his bill and stayed there) …That is worse.

    CAROLYN. It’s worse.

    THOMAS. And the carefulness that got her through—

    CAROLYN. —is the same carefulness people call cold now. She can’t see it either. From the inside, a wall doesn’t feel like a wall. It feels like doing the job properly.

    THOMAS. Then make it right. Put her at the Inner Court. Invite her to everything for a month and let her see.

    CAROLYN. And then?

    THOMAS. And then she is invited.

    CAROLYN. And then she knows why.

    THOMAS. (a pause) Ah.

    CAROLYN. If I hand her a seat because I feel badly, I have told her that a seat is the thing you get when somebody feels badly. Which is what she already believes. I’d be proving her right and calling it an apology.

    THOMAS. …Then what does work?

    CAROLYN. Asking her for something. A gift says I owe you. A question says I need you. Only one of those puts her anywhere.

    THOMAS. There is an old teaching about this, and everyone files it under heaven. A house you did not build. A room made up before you ever got there. Nothing you carry in through the door adds to it, and nothing you leave behind takes it away.

    CAROLYN. I know the one.

    THOMAS. I have begun to think it is also about kitchens.

    CAROLYN. You could put that in the book.

    THOMAS. I could put it in the book.

    CAROLYN. You could also just ask her what the column is for.

    Advisor’s Notes

    Thomas the Drake of Snouton, Storykeeper and Master of Lessons

    What happened

    A travelling baker arrived in a week the kingdom had no oven, and was in the Kitchen Royal with a key by Thursday. A tired sentence said late at night travelled to Marketrow, reached the person it was about, and the record-keeper of twenty years began applying every rule exactly as written.

    Where the line ran

    Marisol Vega told that story warmly, in a new town, to prove she had been let in. She is not sorry and I do not think she ought to be; she was handed something and she carried it the way you carry a thing you were handed. Her Majesty said something true and unfinished to a person she had known four days, which is not a crime, but it is a key, and it went to somebody who had not been in the kingdom long enough to know what it opened. And Miss Quill did not break one rule. She stopped bending them, all in the same hour, having decided to — and then she told us so herself, badly, which took more out of her than the ledger ever did.

    Which brings me to my own part, and I would rather not, and Old Ambrose has made it necessary. He came by with a jar of the dark honey and observed that I had declared a stranger one of us inside four minutes, and had used the word recognise while doing it.

    “You did not recognise her,” he said. “You liked her. Those feel exactly the same from the inside, and only one of them takes any time.”

    I said something about the discernment proper to my office. He ate a great deal of my bread and let me hear it. I am still turning it over, and I notice that I would like to be finished turning it over rather sooner than is honest, so I shall come back to it.

    What Scripture says, and how we got there

    Our Lord has just told a story that makes everybody uncomfortable. A manager is being dismissed for wasting his master's property, and on his way out he goes quietly round the debtors reducing what each of them owes, so that somebody will take him in afterwards. And he is commended — not for honesty, of which he has none, but for having thought one move ahead. Then, with the room still unsettled, our Lord turns from the story to the principle underneath it, and puts the whole question of who should be handed what on a very small scale:

    One who is faithful in a very little is also faithful in much. — Luke 16:10, ESV

    He does not leave it there either. The next line presses harder: if a man has not been faithful with money, which is the small thing, who will entrust him with true riches? That is the word carrying the weight of the passage, and it is the word this episode is about. The question is not who is nice. It is who gets handed something.

    Read the verse quickly and it sounds like encouragement for people doing unglamorous work — chin up, the little things count. Read it slowly and it is a claim about evidence. He does not say small faithfulness is a lesser proof, to be accepted provisionally while we wait for a better one. He says it is the same proof. A person faithful in a very little has already shown you the thing you were trying to find out about them. There is no further test pending.

    Now follow that home, because it goes somewhere uncomfortable. If faithfulness is proved in very little things, then it is proved slowly — not because slowness is a virtue, but for a plain arithmetical reason. Very little things arrive one at a time. There is no way to have a great many of them all at once. A person cannot be on time for you forty times in a fortnight, or keep a confidence you have not yet had occasion to tell them.

    Which means charm is not a shortcut to that proof. This is the part I had wrong, and I had it wrong in a way that felt like discernment. I assumed charm was a weaker sort of evidence — thin, but pointing the same direction, so that four delightful days might stand in for a year of small ones at a discount. It is not weaker evidence. It is a different substance altogether, because charm produces no small things at all. Nothing to count. Nothing to add up. Nothing on the page. Marisol Vega has forty years of very little behind her somewhere, in towns I have never visited. Pigglyvale had four days of it, and handed her a key on the strength of the wrong forty years.

    I can hear the objection, because it is mine. Does this not make a cold and calculating thing out of friendship — everyone on probation, everyone being marked? It would, if what were being rationed were love. It is not. Love is owed to every soul on the road and owed immediately, and our Lord Himself hands out mercy to crowds He does not hand Himself over to. Warmth is free and should be spent freely. What is being weighed is something narrower: not do I care for you, but what may I safely put in your hands. Those two have been confused in this kingdom for years, and confusing them is what makes a person feel that a slow yes is an insult.

    And there is a mercy inside the slowness that is easy to miss. If trust is built out of small things, then the test for it is cheap. You do not need a crisis to find out who somebody is. A Tuesday will do it.

    So to the other side of the same page, and the part I cannot get comfortable about. Miss Quill had twenty years of very little, entered in her own hand, in a column nobody read. The evidence was not missing. It was never missing. It sat in a ledger on a shelf in a room with the window open, in columns her Princess had been initialling since she was nineteen — and the total went unread because reliability makes no noise, and a thing that makes no noise has to be gone and looked for.

    Which is the whole of it, and I will put it as plainly as I can. Trust is not a warmth you extend. It is a sum, and somebody has to do the adding. Delight arrives free and unbidden and asks nothing of you. Attention costs. And in one fortnight this kingdom spent everything it had on the first and nothing at all on the second, and both bills came due on the same Thursday.

    Try it this week

    Find the person whose work you have been quietly living on — the one who has never once dropped it and has therefore never once been noticed — and do not give them a compliment. A compliment is a lovely thing and it closes the subject; it hands them a verdict and asks nothing back. Ask them instead for something only they could tell you:

    “I want your judgement on this, not your permission. Would you sit down a minute?”

    It is a smaller sentence than a speech and it costs a great deal more, because having said it you then have to sit there and hear the answer, and possibly do something about it. That is the whole difference. A compliment says I have noticed you. A question says I need you, and only one of those puts anybody anywhere.

    The Movement Card — Episode Two

    Two movements this fortnight. The big one is drawn with a broken line, because nothing was underneath it — four rings inward in a fortnight, on three delightful weeks and a smell of good bread, and then straight back out to the edge the moment any weight came on. The other one is the tick mark in the corner. It took twenty years, it moved one ring, and it is the only one of the two that will still be there next spring.

    From My Toolbox

    The rent on a room you already live in

    Some people carry a quiet arithmetic: I am allowed to be here because I am useful, and because I am exact. It is rarely said out loud, and the person doing it would not recognise the sentence if you read it back to them. They would simply say they like to do the job properly.

    Watch where it shows in this episode. Being passed over for a stranger did not land on Miss Quill as an insult. It landed as a confirmation — the thing she had always half expected, arriving on schedule. And so she went straight back to the one thing that has never once failed her: the book, kept exactly, to the letter. That is not spite. It is a woman returning to the only floor she has ever found that held.

    Here is the cost, and it is a cruel one. The carefulness that saved her at twenty is the same carefulness that gets called cold at forty. She cannot see the difference from inside it, because from inside it there is no difference: it is the same hand, the same pencil, the same page. Nobody arrives at coldness on purpose. They arrive by keeping doing the thing that worked.

    And then there is the part no better ledger can fix, which is why the pattern is so stubborn. There are two different questions a person can be asking, and they sound almost identical from the outside. Am I useful here? is the first, and a ledger can answer it — that is exactly what a ledger is for, and twenty unbroken years is a very good answer. Am I wanted here? is the second, and no amount of evidence answers it, because it was never an evidence question. So the person keeps producing better and better answers to a question nobody asked, and cannot understand why the ache does not lift, and concludes that the answer must not yet be good enough. And so the columns get neater.

    If a place is bought, it can be lost. A person who believes that will never sit all the way down in a chair, because some part of her is always half standing, ready to go and fetch something. The turn, when it comes, cannot be that she must be more useful. It has to be that the seat was never bought — not by her, not by twenty years, not by anyone at all.

    In the Keep, blood gets you the seat and conduct keeps the keys. That picture is borrowed, and the house it is borrowed from is one where the seat was given before you had done a single thing to deserve it and paid for by somebody else entirely. Which is why being asked to sit down landed on her the way it did. It was not a reward for the ledger. It was the first thing in twenty years that had nothing to do with the ledger.

    Held as an analogy and said to be one: the Keep is a good drawing of standing given rather than earned, and it is only a drawing. God does not take His love back as a key.

    Talk it over

    1. Marisol did nothing wrong and Miss Quill broke no rule, and somebody still got hurt. Where exactly did the harm get made, and by whom?
    2. Carolyn’s sentence at eleven at night was true. Is there such a thing as a true sentence you owe somebody the first hearing of — and how would you know which ones those are?
    3. Who is in your seventh column: the person whose work you have been living on so reliably that you have stopped seeing it? (I have one. I have had him for years and I only worked it out this week, over a jar of somebody else’s honey.)

    Pan Sobao

    From Marisol Vega’s clay oven, three stalls along from Gus — soft, faintly sweet, and worth getting up for.

    • 500g bread flour
    • 300ml warm water
    • 45g sugar
    • 10g salt
    • 7g instant yeast
    • 60g lard (manteca), soft — plus a spoonful more for the tops

    Mix the flour, sugar, salt and yeast, then the water, and knead until it comes together rough and shaggy. Now work the lard in a little at a time, and keep going — sobao means kneaded, and this is the part people skip. Ten minutes by hand. The dough should end up smooth, soft, and faintly shiny, and it should not fight you.

    Rise until doubled, about an hour. Divide into two, roll each into a log about a foot long, seam underneath, and rise again on a lined tray until puffy — another forty minutes.

    Rub the tops with soft lard and cut one long shallow slash down each. Bake at 190°C / 375°F for 25–30 minutes, until deep gold. Rub with a little more lard the second they come out; that is what gives the top its shine and keeps the crust soft.

    Eat warm, split, with butter, or with coffee, or standing up in the kitchen at eleven at night with somebody who has made you laugh.
