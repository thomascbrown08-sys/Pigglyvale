    The Helper

    Somebody did a hard thing for you, kindly, without asking. Nothing was aimed at you. Something was taken anyway.

    The practice yard, after the tables are cleared. Thomas has agreed to wear this one on the grounds that it is his and he ought to know it best. Old Ambrose is sitting on the low wall with a jar of the dark honey, which is generally a sign. Bruno has been told he may not laugh. Bruno is laughing.

    Two drills. Pick whatever you would actually say — not the one you think is being asked for — and see what it caused. Then run it again and try the others, because the point of a yard is that you get to find out without it costing anybody anything.

    Drill one — it is done to you, and you find out after

    Gus Thornapple is three weeks behind on the stall levy and you are the one who has to say so. You have been working up to it since Tuesday, badly. You have rehearsed the first sentence four times and the fourth was worse than the first.

    You come up Marketrow with it sitting in your chest, and it is done. Thomas has already spoken to him. Kindly, and well. The levy will be settled by Friday and Gus was not even sour about it.

    Thomas is pleased. He is pleased for you.

    “Don't thank me. I could see you were dreading it.”

    What do you say?

    What happened next

    Thomas is delighted, and the afternoon is a pleasant one.

    Nine days later there is a thing with Beatrix's apprentice, and he handles it before you get there. A fortnight after that it is Auntie Yolanda and the lamp oil. Each time he is a little quicker off the mark, because each time he has been thanked.

    By midsummer you notice something you cannot quite put down: you have not begun a hard sentence in two months, and nobody in the kingdom expects you to. Gus, for his part, has formed the settled view that when the Princess is displeased with you, she sends a duck.

    Ambrose

    You were not lying. It was kind, and saying so cost you nothing, which is exactly the trouble — a true sentence that costs nothing is often the one doing the most damage.

    Two things went out of the room with that thank-you. You told him it worked, so he will do it again and the next one will be bigger. And you agreed to his account of you: that you were dreading it and needed carrying. That account is not his any more. It is yours as well.

    Left ditch. Caving.

    What happened next

    The feathers on the back of his head go straight up. His spectacles stay exactly where they are, which is how you know he is not undefended but braced.

    “I see,” he says. He says it the way you say it when you do not see at all.

    He is scrupulously polite for four days. He does not stop stepping in. What he stops is mentioning it — and the next time he settles something on your behalf you do not find out for a month, and you find out from Bruno.

    Ambrose

    Every word of that was true, and you got it out of your chest instead of leaving it there to rot for a fortnight. That is worth something and I will not pretend otherwise.

    But it aimed at him rather than at the thing he did, and it told him nothing about what to do differently. So the next ten minutes were about your tone rather than his choice — and you did not stop the behaviour. You drove it underground. A sharp no does that: it does not change what people do, only whether they tell you.

    Right ditch. Lashing.

    What happened next

    You go up the row after the stalls have thinned out and you say it. Gus looks at you with real bewilderment. “The duck already came.”

    You say it anyway. It takes forty seconds. It is smaller than it was in your chest, the way these things almost always are, and you walk home having actually done the thing.

    Three weeks later Thomas does it again. Of course he does. Nothing that happened told him not to.

    And Gus, who owed one levy, has now been spoken to about it twice.

    Ambrose

    Now, that is not nothing. You got back the thing that was taken — the evidence. You found out you could do it, which is precisely what the help had cost you, and you cannot learn that from being told.

    But you paid for it twice and Thomas learned nothing, so you will be standing here again in three weeks. And spare a thought for Gus, who has had one debt and two conversations about it. Doing the right thing privately, where the person who needs to hear it cannot, is a way of being brave without being clear.

    Not a ditch. A half-measure — the road walked alone.

    What happened next

    His spectacles slide down his bill. He does not push them back up.

    “Ah,” he says.

    Then, after a moment in which a great deal visibly happens behind the spectacles: “I did not ask. I did not ask because I was fairly sure of the answer, and I did not want it.”

    Eleven days later Beatrix's apprentice does something foolish with a delivery, and Thomas arrives in the kitchen and stands in the doorway and says, with the appalling formality of a duck doing a new thing badly: “Your Majesty. Do you wish to be asked about this?”

    It is the least graceful sentence he has ever produced. You say yes.

    Ambrose

    Three things in one breath, and none of them optional. The kindness named as real, because it was, and saying so is not a concession. The cost named without a verdict on him attached. And then a request, which is the part most people leave off — it leaves him somewhere to go. There is a version of him that gets this right, and you have just described it to him. He cannot aim at a door you have not shown him.

    Notice too what you did not do. You went to him, and you went to him alone, before it was anybody else's business. Our Lord puts that first for a reason (Matthew 18:15) — not as a procedure to work through, but because a thing said between two people can still be mended by two people.

    There is further along this same road, if you want it. You could have added the shameful part: “I've been dreading it for three weeks and I was ashamed of that, which is why I never mentioned it. Ask me next time — even if I look like I'm drowning.” That costs more, and it gives him something. He was reaching for something real when he stepped in; the short version shuts the door he was knocking on, and the long one opens it and puts him where he can actually be of use.

    The road. And there is more of it.

    Run it again — the others are worth seeing, and nobody is watching.

    All four. Notice that the two ditches look like opposites and are not. One buys you out of the awkwardness; the other buys you out of feeling small. Both are about you. Only the fourth cost you anything on his behalf, which is the whole of how you tell them apart.

    Drill two — it is done to you, in front of everybody

    Same mask, different afternoon. The first one you found out about after it was over. This one happens while you are sitting there.

    The Long Table, Friday. Gus asks down the length of it whether the Pepper Patch is staying common ground or being parcelled out, because he would like to know what he is digging in the spring.

    You have been turning this over all week. You have not decided. You open your mouth.

    “Her Majesty and I discussed it,” says Thomas, comfortably. “The patch stays common ground.”

    You did discuss it. He is not lying. And it is now decided, out loud, in front of forty people, and the table has already nodded and moved on to the bread.

    What do you do?

    What happened next

    The table moves on. Nobody notices anything, because nothing visible happened.

    You speak to him on the Sunday and he is genuinely sorry and says so properly. And then on the Monday Gus starts digging on the assumption the patch is common ground, because that is what he heard on Friday, and by the time you have decided otherwise there are two rows of onions in it.

    You let the onions stand. It was easier than explaining.

    Ambrose

    Taking it to him alone was right and I would not have you stop doing it. But notice what a private repair cannot reach: the sentence was said in public, so it went on working in public while you were waiting for Sunday.

    Some things can be mended afterwards and some things have already left the room. A decision announced at a table of forty is the second kind. Sunday was the right conversation at the wrong hour.

    Left ditch — the polite version of caving.

    What happened next

    “Thomas — I'd like to answer that one.”

    His spectacles slide. “Of course, Majesty.”

    And then you have to answer it, which is the part you had been avoiding all week, and you answer it badly — you say you have not decided, and that you will say on Monday, and that you would like to hear what Gus thinks first. Gus is so surprised at being asked that he has to go and stand behind a wall about it.

    Nobody at the table thinks less of anybody. It takes eleven seconds.

    Ambrose

    Eleven seconds, and every part of it is public, which is where the thing happened. You did not contradict him and you did not correct him — you simply took the floor back, which was always yours, and he handed it over without a wound because you asked for the thing rather than objecting to him.

    And notice the cost, because it is not the awkwardness. It is that you then had to stand there with an unfinished decision in front of the kingdom. He stepped in precisely to spare you that, and taking the floor back means taking the exposure with it. There is no version of this where you get the standing and he keeps the discomfort.

    The road.

    What happened next

    The table goes quiet in the particular way a table does when two people at the head of it are not agreeing.

    Thomas says, “Ah — forgive me,” and is perfectly gracious about it, and the bread comes round, and it is fine.

    And for the next month he checks with you before he says anything at all, including things that plainly do not need checking, in a way that is scrupulously correct and slightly cold, and twice you catch yourself wishing he would just answer.

    Ambrose

    It was true and it was necessary and it was said at the right moment, so I will not call it a ditch. It was aimed a hand's breadth wrong, that is all.

    I hadn't decided that corrects him. I'd like to answer that one claims the floor. They achieve the same thing in the room and they leave very different men standing there afterwards — and the difference costs you nothing, which is why it is worth the half-second it takes to choose.

    Right of the road. Barely, and it still cost a month.

    What happened next

    Gus nods and goes back to his plate. It has been handled, briskly, and the table never even pauses.

    But you have said that is Thomas's view in front of forty people, which puts a small distance between the two of you on a matter he had thought you were agreed on — and he had some reason to think that, because you did discuss it.

    He does not mention it. He is quieter on the walk back than he was on the walk down.

    Ambrose

    Efficient, and you got the decision back, and you did it without an argument. That is three real things.

    But you separated yourself from him publicly in order to do it, and that is a larger coin than the situation required. He spoke out of turn; he did not turn out to be a stranger. Watch for this one — it is the move that feels like calm boundary-setting and lands as distance.

    Not a ditch. The road, taken at somebody else's expense.

    Run it again. The difference between the second and third answers is the whole of this drill.

    All four. Set this beside drill one. There you had all evening to choose your sentence and the cost was awkwardness. Here you had four seconds and the cost was standing in public with an unfinished decision. Same mask, and the thing that got harder was not the wording.

    Now the other side of it

    Those were the easy half, and they are the half everybody wants. Here are the two that matter.

    Drill three — you are wearing it, and you can see her struggling

    Pim has to tell Gus Thornapple that his egg delivery was two short, and that it was two short last week as well.

    She has had her apron on since after lunch. She is by the kitchen door. She has been by the kitchen door for eleven minutes, and twice she has got as far as the step.

    You are at the worktable. You can see all of this.

    What do you do?

    What happened next

    Her whole body loosens. She is so relieved that she thanks you twice, and the second one is the one that ought to bother you.

    You go. It takes four minutes. Gus is fine about it.

    In the autumn there is a thing with a delivery of flour, and Pim comes and finds you before she has been to the door at all. She does not stand there for eleven minutes any more. She has learned the shorter route.

    Ambrose

    You will notice that nothing unkind happened anywhere in that. It was quick, she was grateful, Gus was fine, and you were good at it.

    And you have taught her that the way through a hard conversation is to find somebody larger. She was two steps from finding out she could do it. That was the thing she was about to gain and you spent it for her, in four minutes, for the relief on her face.

    This is the mask. It is on you, and it fits beautifully.

    What happened next

    She looks up at you with enormous gratitude and you walk down together.

    And then, at the stall, one of two things happens, and which one it is has nothing to do with what you intended.

    Either you stand slightly behind her and say nothing at all while she gets it out — badly, and with one restart, and entirely by herself.

    Or Gus turns and looks at you, because you are the Princess and you are standing there, and it is the most natural thing in the world to answer him. And then it was your conversation, and she was present at it.

    Ambrose

    Going with somebody is not the same as going instead of them, and I would not have you think it is. Company is a real gift. Half the courage in this kingdom is borrowed from whoever happened to be standing nearby.

    But this one turns on a thing you have not decided yet, and if you do not decide it before you get there, the room will decide it for you. Are you going as her company, or as her cover? So settle it on the walk down, and say it out loud: I'll come, and I'm not going to say anything. Then she has both — somebody beside her, and the thing still hers.

    Not a ditch, and not yet the road. It depends entirely on the next four minutes.

    What happened next

    She stands there a while longer. Then she goes.

    She comes back twenty minutes later having done it, and she does not mention it, and you do not mention it either, and she is a fraction taller for a week.

    But there was a version of that afternoon where she stood at the door until it got dark and then quietly took her apron off, and you would not have known, because you were chopping.

    Ambrose

    It worked. And it worked partly because you were right about her and partly because you were lucky, and from where you were standing those two look identical.

    The difficulty with silence is that it is the same shape as neglect. It leaves her alone with it, which is what she needed, and it also leaves her alone with it, which is the risk. If you are going to be quiet, be quiet and available — where she can see you, not busy enough to be unreachable.

    Sometimes the road. Sometimes just not looking.

    What happened next

    “On my own,” she says immediately, and then looks faintly appalled at herself for having said it so fast.

    “Right,” you say, and go back to the board.

    She is gone twenty-five minutes. When she comes back she says Gus said it was the geese and not him, and that she said that it was still two short either way, and you can hear in the way she reports it that she has already told herself the story twice on the walk home.

    You do not make anything of it. You ask her to check the onions.

    Ambrose

    One sentence, and it did the whole job. It is not a small thing that she got to answer it — you handed her the decision along with the offer, which is the difference between help and management.

    And you will notice what it cost you. You had to be willing to hear yes, come with me, and mean it. An offer you are hoping will be refused is not an offer. That is the price of this one, and it is why it is the road: you gave up the outcome.

    The road. It is one sentence long and it is not easy.

    Run it again. This one has no clean answers in it, which is the point of it.

    All four. Only one of them was certainly wrong, and it was the kind one. That is the whole of what this belt is for: the mask is not cruelty wearing a smile, it is love with a fault in it, and it fits so well because most of what is underneath is real.

    Drill four — you are wearing it, and nobody is struggling yet

    Drill three was a rescue. This one is a prediction, which is much harder to catch, because nothing has gone wrong yet and you are only being sensible.

    Bruno is putting the lantern-oil contract to Miss Quill on Thursday. You know exactly how that will go. He will arrive without figures and be charming about it; she will ask him for the figures; he will improvise; and she will thank him for his time in under four minutes.

    It is Tuesday. You could have a quiet word with Quill. You could have a quiet word with Bruno. You have not been asked to do either.

    What do you do?

    What happened next

    Thursday goes well. Miss Quill is patient in a way she is not usually patient, asks him for the figures twice instead of once, and he gets there in the end. He comes out delighted with himself.

    He tells the story for a fortnight. In his version he read her exactly right.

    In the spring he takes the same approach to the bread contract with Beatrix, who has had no quiet word from anybody, and it goes the way Thursday would have gone.

    Ambrose

    Nobody was deceived and nothing untrue was said. You put a thumb on a scale that was not yours, and you did it invisibly, which is the part to sit with.

    He now believes a thing about himself that is not true, and he believes it on your evidence. He will act on it. And when it fails in the spring he will not be able to work out why, because the information he needs — that Thursday was carried — is the one thing you cannot tell him now without taking Thursday away from him retrospectively.

    This is the mask at its most comfortable. It never has to be defended, because nobody ever finds out.

    The mask. The invisible fitting.

    What happened next

    It goes exactly as you predicted. Four minutes. He comes out looking like a man who has walked into a door.

    On the Monday he asks Pim what a ledger column is for. In June he puts the same contract up again with three years of figures on two sheets of paper, and Miss Quill approves it in eleven minutes and says, to nobody, that it is a decent piece of work.

    You had nothing to do with any of that, which is the point of it.

    Ambrose

    Sometimes the road is a closed mouth, and I would rather say so plainly than pretend every answer here is a sentence.

    But be careful how you hold it, because doing nothing is the same shape as not caring, and only you know which one you did. If you stayed out because it was his to walk, that is respect. If you stayed out because you rather wanted to see it, that is a different afternoon wearing the same face. And if he had asked you on the Wednesday and you had still said nothing, it would have been neither.

    Often the road. Check your reason for it.

    What happened next

    He listens. He takes the figures. Thursday goes well and he is generous about where the help came from.

    And a small thing settles that does not settle back. The next time he has something to put to anybody, he comes to you first — not for the figures, but to find out whether you think it will land. By the autumn he is checking his own ideas with you before he is sure he has them.

    Ambrose

    Now, this is a good deal better than the first answer, and I want that said. It was open, he knew it was happening, and he kept his own Thursday.

    What it skipped was two seconds long. You decided he needed it. He did not get to decide, and he did not know there had been a decision, so nothing about it looked like a decision to him either — it just looked like you being useful, which is exactly how a habit gets made.

    Coaching somebody who has not asked is still telling them they were about to need you. Much gentler than doing it for them. Same sentence underneath.

    Not a ditch. The road with the asking left out.

    What happened next

    “Cold,” he says, immediately, with enormous confidence.

    Thursday takes four minutes and he comes out looking like a man who has walked into a door.

    And on the Friday he finds you, and says — not sheepishly, which is the interesting part, but like somebody placing an order — right, go on then, how does she read a proposal?

    Ambrose

    One sentence, and it did three things at once. It offered. It handed him the decision. And it told him, without a word of warning in it, that there was something to know — which is why he came back on Friday rather than deciding she was unreasonable.

    You will notice it cost you Thursday. You had to watch it, having offered, which is harder than watching it having said nothing. That is the price of the asking: you have to be willing to hear cold and mean it. An offer you are hoping will be accepted is not an offer either.

    The road. Both halves of it — the offer, and letting him refuse.

    Run it again. The first and third answers are the two most people would call kind.

    All four. Set this beside drill three. There she was standing at the door with her apron on and you could see it. Here nothing had happened yet and you were only being sensible — and the mask fits better in this one, because there is no moment where it looks like rescue.

    Taking it off

    Almost nobody wearing this one thinks they are. It does not feel like taking. It feels like love, and the reason it feels like love is that most of it is love. That is what makes it a mask rather than a lie — the wearer is the first one fooled.

    So the question is not do I help too much. That question has no answer and everybody answers it wrong. The question is narrower, and it has a real answer:

    When you stepped in — did you ask her first? And if you didn't, what were you afraid would happen if you had?

    Sit with the second half. The honest answer is usually she would have said no, and then I would have had to stand there and watch. Which means the help was not only for her. Some of it was for you, and the asking got skipped precisely because asking risked an answer you did not want.

    That is not a monstrous thing to find out about yourself. It is an extremely ordinary one. But it does mean the repair is not helping less. It is one sentence, before:

    “Would you like me to, or would you rather do it yourself?”

    And when you have already done it — and you will, because everyone does — the mask comes off out loud, with the parts named:

    “I did that without asking you. I did it because watching you struggle is hard for me, and that is my reason and it is not an excuse. What would you like me to do now?”

    Which is roughly what Thomas said in Episode One, standing in the doorway of the Kitchen Royal, after a four-year-old with a basket of eggs asked him the question he had never once thought to ask himself.

    Practise it

    1. Say drill one's fourth answer out loud, twice, until it stops sounding rude. It is not rude. It only feels that way because the first half is a compliment and your mouth expects the second half to be one too.
    2. Think of the last time somebody helped you in a way that left you smaller. What would you have wanted them to do instead? Say that sentence now, as though they were in the room.
    3. The harder one, and the one worth actually doing. Think of the last time you did it to somebody. Did you ask?
