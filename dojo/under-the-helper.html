    The Dojo · Under a Mask

    Under the Helper

    What it protects, what it buys, why it fails — and what it looks like on your own face.

    What is under it

    “I was protecting you.”

    What it protects underneath
    Not you. Them. Watching somebody you love struggle is genuinely painful, and standing there useless while it happens is worse. The mask lifts both at once.
    What it buys
    Real goods, and they should be said plainly. The hard thing is done. You are spared an afternoon of dread. And they get to be useful to somebody they love, which is one of the better feelings available to a person.
    Why it fails
    It answers a need they have and charges it to you. And what it takes is not the errand — it is the evidence. You were about to find out you could do it. Now you will not, and next time the dread will be the same size or bigger, because nothing has happened to shrink it.
    What Scripture puts in its place
    Paul says bear one another's burdens, and then four verses later that each will have to bear his own load — which looks like a contradiction until you notice he has used two different words. The first is a crushing weight, more than one person can carry. The second is a man's own pack, the ordinary size of thing he is meant to walk under. The command is to get under what would crush somebody. It is not to carry their pack, and doing it anyway is not obedience to the first verse but disobedience to the second. Lifting a burden is love. Lifting a load says you could not have. — Galatians 6:2, 6:5

    Taking it off your own face

    Almost nobody wearing this one thinks they are. It does not feel like taking. It feels like love, and the reason it feels like love is that most of it is love. That is what makes it a mask rather than a lie — the wearer is the first one fooled.

    The useful question is narrow. Not do I help too much, which nobody answers honestly, but: when you stepped in, did you ask her first — and if you didn't, what were you afraid would happen if you had? The honest answer is usually she would have said no, and then I would have had to stand there and watch. Which means the help was not only for her.

    So the repair is not helping less. It is one sentence, before:

    “Would you like me to, or would you rather do it yourself?”

    And when you have already done it — and you will, because everyone does — the mask comes off out loud, with the parts named: “I did that without asking you. I did it because watching you struggle is hard for me, and that is my reason and it is not an excuse. What would you like me to do now?”

    Which is roughly what Thomas said in Episode One, after a four-year-old with a basket of eggs asked him the question he had never once thought to ask himself.

    If you want the clinical words for it

    If you would rather have the terms, Codependency on My Toolbox names the mechanism this mask runs on — and Nowhere safe to fail is worth reading beside it, because a person who has never been allowed to fail safely will be helped out of every chance to try.

    If you want to go further with this

    Keep Your Heart has a whole chapter on this mask, arrived at independently and from the same two verses: Burdens and Loads — carrying what was never yours and refusing help with what is; these look like opposite faults, and they are one. Read Care That Was Not Care beside it for the line that decides this whole mask: care is glad when you no longer need it.

    Where it turns up

    One oddity worth knowing about, because it catches people who have understood everything else. When you watch somebody else wear this mask — a friend covering for somebody, an aunt answering a question that was not put to her — the obvious move is to step in and stop it. Which is stepping in. You would be taking a thing out of somebody's hands in order to object to a thing being taken out of somebody's hands, and it does not stop being that because your reason is better. The way through is the same as everywhere else on this page: hand the floor back to the person it belonged to, rather than holding it for them yourself.

    This one sits low and stays low. It is worn in good faith and it does not climb the belts easily — a helper who will not stop when asked has stopped being a helper, and what you are looking at is a different mask.
