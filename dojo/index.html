    A Wing of the Kingdom

    The Dojo

    The practice yard, where the kingdom rehearses the conversations it is bad at — slowly, out loud, and with somebody laughing in the background.

    The episodes are stories. Things happen in them and nobody is practising.

    This is the other thing. Here the characters put on a mask, run a scene, and stop halfway through to argue about it. None of it happened. Bruno has never spoken to anybody the way he speaks in the yellow belt cases and would be appalled to be told he had. He is acting, badly, and enjoying it more than is decent.

    Every case works the same way. A short scene, and one sentence that lands on you. Then four things you might say — you pick one, and you find out what it caused. Not a verdict: an actual afternoon, three weeks later, with the consequence in it. Then Old Ambrose says what he thinks, and only then does he put a name to what you did.

    The names are kept until afterwards on purpose. If the answers were labelled you would pick the one labelled correct, learn nothing, and go away with a script. Choose what you would really say, run it again, and try the others — that is what a yard is for. Nothing here costs anybody anything.

    Every case ends with the same mask on your face, because that is the half nobody wants and the half that is worth the paper.

    Two ditches and a road

    Everything here runs on one shape, so it is worth having it before you start.

    Left is caving. You agree, you smooth it over, you say it is fine. It buys you a quiet evening.

    Right is lashing. You say the true thing with the edge on it. It buys you the relief of not being a doormat.

    They look like opposites and they are not. Both are ways of protecting yourself — one from the awkwardness, one from feeling small. The road is neither, and it is not the average of them either. It is the response that costs you something for the other person's sake: true, said so that they can hear it, and leaving them a way back. Which is why it is usually the hardest of the three and never the most moderate.

    If a response gets easier as it gets better, it is not the road.

    The belts

    The belts are not ranked by how badly the other person is behaving. They are ranked by how much benefit of the doubt the evidence will still bear — how much room is left to explain what happened kindly, and still be honest.

    Two things raise a belt, and neither of them is a guess about somebody's motive.

    A track record. The same move, again, and again, and it lands the same way every time and always in their favour. One of anything proves nothing. Six of a thing is a shape.

    An act that is out of bounds on its own. Something wrong in itself, whatever was meant by it — a confidence repeated to other people, a checkable fact denied, somebody's standing moved as a punishment. These can raise a belt inside a single conversation, because you do not need a pattern to know that a thing happened.

    Notice that both of those are acts. That is deliberate. “You told three people what I said in confidence” is checkable and can be answered. “You're manipulative” is a verdict on an inside you have never seen. So a higher belt changes how carefully you respond and what happens to their keys — and it still tells you nothing about their heart, which stays shut to you at every belt on this page.

    The same mask therefore turns up at several belts. What moves it up is never the mask. It is what else is in the room.

    • White — nothing was aimed

      They were tired, clumsy, frightened, or in a hurry. The sting was real. The intent was not there at all.

    • Yellow — aimed at the problem, not at you

      Ordinary pressure. A sharp ask, an argument pressed hard, somebody who wants a thing and is saying so.

    • Green — defending themselves badly

      Their own shame is driving it. Good faith underneath, genuine harm on top, and they cannot see the second because of the first.

    • Brown — a pattern you can no longer explain charitably

      Repeated, shaped, and it benefits them every time. You still do not know their heart, and you are not required to guess at it in order to act.

    • Black — the mask has fused

      They are no longer telling you a story. They are telling themselves one, and they believe it, which is why nothing you say reaches. Technique runs out here. What is left is whether you will say the true thing and pay for it.

    In the actual art a black belt means a beginner — somebody who has finished learning the basics and can now start. That is about right.

    The masks

    A mask is not a personality. It is a sentence a person tells themselves so that what they are doing can go on feeling alright. That is why it is a mask and not a lie: most of what is underneath is real, and the wearer is the one being fooled first.

    Masks are not sorted by belt. The same one turns up at white belt worn for an afternoon and at brown belt worn for twenty years, and watching one travel up the belts is most of what there is to learn about it.

    • The Helper — “I was protecting you.” Protects: the pain of watching someone you love struggle, and of being useless while it happens. White belt, case one →
    • The Peacemaker — “I was being kind.” Protects: from watching somebody's face fall in front of you.
    • The Softener — “No, honestly, it's alright.” Protects: from finding out whether the friendship survives the truth.
    • The Rulebook — “I'm only following the rule.” Protects: from a place that feels contingent on being exact.
    • The Expert — “I'm the one who knows about this.” Protects: from being unnecessary.
    • The Joke — “I was only teasing.” Protects: from being disliked for having said the real thing plainly.
    • The Ledger — “After everything I've done for you.” Protects: from the fear of being loved for what you give rather than for yourself.
    • The Standard — “I'm just honest. I have high standards.” Protects: from being ordinary at warmth — so the shortfall gets renamed a virtue.
    • The Mind-Reader — “I know how you really feel about me.” Protects: from having to ask a question that might be answered no. Green belt, case one →
    • The Turn — “I can't believe you'd accuse me of that.” Protects: from ever being the one who did something. You raised a small true thing and twenty minutes later you are apologising.

    Five of those are already on this site, worn by people you like. That is not an accident and it is not a criticism of them. It is the whole argument for a practice yard: if the masks only ever turned up on villains, nobody would need to learn them.
